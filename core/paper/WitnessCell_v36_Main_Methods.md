# WitnessCell predicts combinatorial perturbation responses through endpoint and partner learning

## Abstract

Joint perturbation measurements can improve combination prediction by refining individual intervention contributions or learning partner-dependent changes. WitnessCell predicts unmeasured responses from single and joint measurements, distinguishes these contributions and constructs separately testable partner corrections. Controlled causal analysis shows how joint-intervention layouts resolve ambiguities about unmeasured targets. The released predictor ranks first among sixteen entries in a four-dataset benchmark, including double perturbations. A functional procedure fixed before Jurkat fitting reduces complete-response and partner-contrast errors by 6.43% and 6.18% across 617 held-out pairs. RNA readout shrinkage reduces adverse corrections while retaining concentrated benefits. The response decomposition further enables selective control: internal held-combination measurements set partner strength while endpoint contributions are preserved. Target-side calibration retains small useful increments in selected frozen partner functions. WitnessCell connects competitive response prediction with explicit partner learning and targeted use of the resulting corrections.

Combinatorial perturbation screens measure how a cell's response to one intervention changes when another is added. Predicting unmeasured combinations would extend these screens and help researchers study gene function across cellular contexts.[1,2,3] A practical starting point is a collection of single-perturbation measurements and a partially measured combination screen. The immediate goal is to predict the remaining expression or functional responses. However, a reduction in overall prediction error leaves an important question open: did the model estimate each intervention's contribution more accurately, or did it learn changes that depend on the partner?

Existing methods combine intervention representations, transform cellular states or use gene relationships to predict responses.[4,5,6,7,8] Large-scale benchmarks and strong simple references test whether these predictions improve on alternatives.[9,10] Systema examines whether predictions capture perturbation-specific changes beyond systematic variation, while Miller and colleagues test whether evaluation metrics distinguish informative predictions.[11,12] For combinations, a further question concerns the source of an improvement. Measurements may reveal that a model consistently underestimates one intervention's contribution across several partners. Correcting that shared error improves prediction, but leaves open whether the model also predicts how exchanging partners changes the response in different backgrounds.

Consider replacing partner B with D, first alongside intervention A and then alongside C. If the replacement has different effects in the two backgrounds, predicting that difference requires more than refining the separate contributions of A, B, C and D. We call this difference a partner contrast. It gives a concrete way to ask what a model has learned from joint measurements: better estimates of contributions shared across combinations, or better predictions of changes specific to the pairing.

WitnessCell addresses this distinction within a prediction method. Its core combines single responses nonlinearly and learns a correction from measured combinations. We show that this correction refines contributions associated with individual intervention identities, termed endpoints, while leaving partner contrasts unchanged. We then construct additional corrections that can change those contrasts and test them against the same fitted baseline. A controlled causal system examines the information needed for this task: the choice of joint measurements can determine whether mechanisms giving different answers to an unmeasured combination can be distinguished.[13]

WitnessCell further uses this distinction to control learned predictions: it retains the endpoint contribution of an added correction and determines the strength of its partner component from internal held-out combination measurements. The four-dataset benchmark tests complete prediction; functional and RNA evaluations then test the additional partner learners against their own fitted baselines. K562 and Jurkat examine functional gains under expanded experimental support and a fixed fitting procedure, while the RNA analysis examines training-measurement shrinkage across transcripts. Shen and Han provide complementary tests of using learned corrections: retaining endpoint contributions during attenuation, and calibrating an existing partner direction using target-study measurements. The supplied interfaces support response fitting, missing-combination prediction and component-specific adjustment.

## Results

### WitnessCell separates response components and controls partner corrections

WitnessCell starts from control and available single-perturbation measurements, observed combinations and the identities of requested combinations. Its transcriptomic core, the configuration used in the benchmark, uses measured single effects when available and estimates missing ones from training responses and gene annotations. It combines the single effects through a saturation rule, then transfers residuals from measured combinations that share endpoints with the query (Fig. 1a). Training or validation measurements determine the fitted settings. All measurements from each test combination are excluded from model fitting. The resulting predictor can use combination data even when some requested single effects have not been measured.

Nonlinear composition allows the joint prediction to depart from the sum of the single responses. Residual transfer then estimates the errors associated with particular endpoints across measured combinations. For example, several combinations containing A may consistently exceed the nonlinear prediction; the residual branch can transfer an A-associated correction to another pair containing A. Such a correction can improve the complete response, meaning the prediction across all readouts before any endpoint removal. Whether it also improves partner contrasts depends on how that correction changes when partners are exchanged.

To identify what the transferred correction learns, consider four combinations: A with B or D, and C with B or D. The contrast

$$\Delta=Y_{AB}-Y_{AD}-Y_{CB}+Y_{CD}$$

asks whether exchanging B for D has the same effect in backgrounds A and C. Any contribution depending only on A, B, C or D cancels. Estimating those contributions more accurately can therefore improve complete responses while leaving this contrast unchanged. An endpoint contribution describes the intervention's contribution across all measured readouts, including downstream genes; it is broader than the targeted gene's own RNA response.

The cancellation can be seen by writing an endpoint-only response as $Y_{AB}=a_A+a_B$. The contribution $a_A$ enters both AB and AD and disappears in their difference; $a_C$ disappears in the corresponding CB and CD difference. Both differences then equal $a_B-a_D$, which also cancels when the backgrounds are compared. A nonzero contrast records a background-dependent change in the partner exchange. This comparison removes a separate contribution for each endpoint, whereas subtracting one common response across all perturbations removes only their shared change. The contrast describes the measured response pattern; identifying its molecular mechanism requires additional information.

This cancellation extends to a collection of query pairs. Let $X_Q$ indicate the two endpoints present in each pair. Classical projection gives $P_Q=I-X_QX_Q^+$, which removes sums of endpoint contributions.[14,15] The released residual branch has the form $\delta_Q=X_Q\Theta$, so

$$P_Q\delta_Q=0.$$

The identity establishes an exact limitation of this endpoint-based residual-transfer branch: it cannot change the endpoint-eliminated part of the prediction. The nonlinear baseline can already produce partner contrasts. The added learner must therefore be evaluated against that actual baseline, so that any improvement concerns new predictions beyond those already supplied by nonlinear composition (Fig. 1b,d).

The projector removes the best-fitting sum of endpoint contributions across all query pairs. Rectangles and longer cycles can support the remaining contrasts. The query graph therefore determines which response directions can be tested; its contrast dimension is not a biological sample size.

WitnessCell adds corrections through separately fitted RNA and functional learners (Fig. 1c). The RNA learner uses similarity between the two single-response profiles of a query and those of measured pairs, allowing either endpoint ordering. It removes endpoint contributions from the training problem before estimating the correction. The primary functional learner fits a signed symmetric matrix to residual growth responses, sharing patterns across partners. Shared functions of single effects and gene features provide another functional implementation when query endpoints lack source-specific matrix parameters. These constructions can change partner contrasts. Their complete outputs can also contain endpoint-associated changes; projected evaluation isolates the part relevant to partner prediction.

Being able to change a contrast is only the first requirement for improving it. The correction must point toward the baseline's remaining error and have an appropriate magnitude. Moreover, a correction can improve endpoint contributions while harming partner contrasts. Selecting its strength by complete-response error can favor the former improvement despite the latter damage. This motivates controlling the two parts separately, rather than assigning them one common strength.

For a baseline $M_0$ and learned correction $D$, WitnessCell can retain the endpoint part and adjust only the partner part:

$$\widehat Y_\alpha=M_0+(I-P_Q)D+\alpha P_QD.$$

Internal held-combination predictions determine $\alpha$ between zero and one (Fig. 1e). The endpoint contribution is unchanged. Under the same squared-error measure, any reduction in partner error relative to the original predictor produces an equal reduction in complete-error sum of squares. This exact property separates the operation's target from its predictive success: held-out outcomes still determine whether the selected strength helps. Supplementary Note 17 gives the derivation and explains why the optimal complete-response strength can differ from the optimal partner strength.

Users choose a configuration from their measurements and prediction objective before examining query outcomes. Normalized RNA summaries support the core; training cell halves additionally support the RNA partner extension; repeated functional measurements support the growth model. Existing shared functional predictors can instead supply a frozen correction direction for target-side calibration. These implementations expose a baseline, an added correction and their permitted fitting information; they retain their own parameters and training objectives. The control interface adjusts a specified query panel without its measured outcomes. Supplementary Table S29 specifies inputs and outputs, and Table S30 records the supporting experiments' evaluation status.

### Joint interventions resolve target ambiguities left by single perturbations

We first asked whether the training measurements determine the partner response of interest. We examined this requirement in a controlled causal system with a source, intervened mediators and a downstream response. A direct source effect and an effect passing through a hidden mediator gate can produce identical responses under control and all permitted single knockouts. Knocking out an appropriate pair breaks that agreement: the gate turns off while the source remains active (Fig. 2a). The joint intervention reveals a difference that additional observations of the same single knockouts cannot resolve.

Two mechanisms can fit all observed conditions equally well yet predict different unmeasured responses. Selecting one of them does not remove that ambiguity from the data. Conversely, the mechanisms need not agree about every internal parameter for a particular query to become predictable: it is sufficient that they agree on the requested response. We therefore examine identification of the query, rather than recovery of the entire causal network. This makes the theoretical question correspond to the prediction task used throughout the study.

We compared two sets of joint measurements while keeping the number of conditions, observations and appearances of every endpoint identical. Both excluded the same four query pairs (Fig. 2b). In the comparison set, four possible mechanisms generated identical training distributions but disagreed on the requested partner contrast. This imposes an estimator-independent squared-error floor of 0.0625. Repeating those conditions more precisely preserves the ambiguity. The informative set instead distinguished the mechanisms responsible for the different query answers (Fig. 2c).

The panels differ in which mediators are intervened on together. Matching endpoint appearances and observation budgets separates this choice from observing particular mediators more often or repeating conditions more precisely. The informative layout exposes a response route hidden under the comparison layout.

Changing only the measurement set reduced target error by 92.84% for the same least-squares procedure (Fig. 2d). A strong generalized least-squares estimator also benefited from the informative measurements. The gain therefore reflects what the experiments reveal about the query, rather than an advantage exclusive to one estimator. Identification depends on the specified mechanism family; allowing additional simultaneous gates restores ambiguity, as shown by a counterexample in Supplementary Note 2.

The experiment shows why endpoint coverage and observation count alone do not determine the information available for an unmeasured combination. Which interventions are measured together can matter even at the same budget. The biological analyses next ask how WitnessCell uses available combination measurements, first to improve complete predictions and then to learn additional partner-dependent responses.

### WitnessCell leads aggregate evaluation of unmeasured combinations

We evaluated the WitnessCell core in the genetic-combination track of scPerturBench, using its four datasets and original six-metric aggregation.[9] The comparison included fifteen eligible comparator entries, including simple references, alongside WitnessCell. The complete track combines double perturbations and unseen singles; we also evaluated the 540 double-perturbation records separately, retaining the same methods, scores and aggregation rule. These datasets include combinatorial expression, CaRPool, primary T-cell and direct-capture perturbation measurements.[1,16,2,17]

WitnessCell ranked first in both the complete track and the double-only subset. For doubles, its mean operation rank was 3.515, compared with 5.150 for the next-ranked method, Scouter.[18] The paired difference had a 95% cluster-bootstrap interval of −2.117 to −1.141 (Fig. 3a,c). All fifteen comparator differences had pointwise intervals below zero. WitnessCell also ranked first by point estimate when neither, one or both query endpoints had been seen in training (Fig. 3b). Complete rankings and paired intervals for these subsets of the benchmark are reported in Supplementary Tables S25–S26.

The aggregate comparison summarizes ranks across metrics, while MSE measures error magnitude in each dataset's original response units. These views reveal different strengths. WitnessCell led the combined evaluation in three datasets and ranked fourth in Schmidt. Its MSE was lower than Scouter's in Norman and Replogle_exp6 but higher in Wessels and Schmidt (Fig. 3d). Wasserstein and common differential-expression rankings were also weaker, and the linear model led on unseen singles. The aggregate lead thus coexists with settings in which other methods predict particular response properties more accurately.

Dataset-specific MSE retains error magnitudes that rank aggregation discards. Distributional scores also have a defined scope: the benchmark combines predicted means with training-control variation, so they evaluate that adapted prediction rather than a directly learned distribution of perturbed cell states.

Additional analyses examined how the core uses combination measurements. Improvements persisted after removing directly manipulated RNA coordinates, showing that downstream readouts benefited. Holding the nonlinear baseline fixed and increasing the available Wessels combination measurements improved complete-response accuracy (Supplementary Fig. S3). This comparison isolates the contribution of the residual library from changes to the single-response composition. Supplementary Note 15 gives the effect sizes and separately defined training-mean reference analysis; Methods and Table S31 describe the historical scoring differences between official reference entries and the sensitivity to their inclusion.

The same saved predictions also confirmed the endpoint identity above: residual transfer left the projected partner error unchanged within numerical precision (Fig. 1d; Supplementary Table S9). Thus, a useful improvement in complete prediction can occur without an additional partner correction. This observation motivates a direct test of whether an explicit partner learner improves unmeasured functional responses.

### Partner learning and component control improve functional-screen completion

Growth measurements test the added learner against a directly measured cellular phenotype. Using the Horlbeck genetic-interaction screen,[3] WitnessCell fits repeat-specific baselines to single and combination measurements, then predicts residual growth through a shared signed matrix. Rank one is the primary correction; a spectral model provides a secondary comparison.[19] Each is tested against its corresponding refitted baseline. Fitness prediction has precedents, including GEARS;[8] the question here is whether explicit partner learning improves a functional predictor that already uses combination measurements.

During K562 development, we compared nested training pools while keeping each fold's query pairs fixed. The low pool contained 421–422 pairs and the high pool 2,317–2,318. Baseline and correction were refitted at each budget. The primary partner gain changed from approximately zero to 11.68%; complete-response gain at high support was 11.03% (Fig. 4a). Comparing with a same-budget baseline isolates the additional correction from improvements obtained simply by giving the baseline more data. Counts, endpoint coverage and layout expanded together; these two settings establish a benefit of expanded support without locating a sample-size threshold. Supplementary Notes 7 and 11 retain the full development history.

We next fixed the model family and selection rules before fitting Jurkat. The primary correction reduced complete-response error by 6.43% and partner error by 6.18% across 617 held-out pairs (Fig. 4b), with positive gains in both measured repeats. Coefficients were estimated anew from 2,464 measured pairs, approximately 80% of possible pairs among 79 endpoints. This tests transfer of the fitting procedure between cellular environments within one study and supports completion of an already substantially measured screen.

The TTI2/ACTR10 backgrounds crossed with PGK1/OGFOD1 make the added prediction concrete. Their observed repeat-mean growth contrast was −0.0581. The correction moved the baseline contrast from −0.0233 to −0.0435 (Fig. 4c,d). Comparing two partners only in the TTI2 background would retain their individual contributions. Subtracting the same exchange in ACTR10 cancels those contributions, isolating the response difference that endpoint refinement cannot change. Both repeats and every eligible continuation of this fixed example are reported in Supplementary Table S35; the full query set supplies the performance estimate.

We then applied the fixed functional procedure to the independent Shen study, using growth responses reconstructed separately for HeLa, A549 and 293T from repeated dual-guide count trajectories.[20] The original correction improved complete-response prediction in all three backgrounds but slightly increased partner error. The same tension appeared in internal validation: complete-response selection favored corrections whose partner alignment was weak relative to their magnitude. Thus, the added branch could change partner responses, but its strength was selected for a broader objective. Supplementary Note 18 preserves the source preparation and original outcomes.

To act on this distinction, we fitted a partner-specific coefficient from saved internal held-combination predictions and compared four outputs: baseline, original correction, whole-correction attenuation and component-specific control. The two attenuation schemes used exactly the same coefficient. Their partner predictions were therefore identical, while only component control preserved the endpoint contribution. This comparison covered all 55 eligible functional configurations, including zero-selected models and K562 layouts sharing queries. It is a retrospective extension of the recorded evaluations.

In Shen, whole-correction attenuation reduced partner damage but also removed much of the original complete-response benefit. Component control retained that endpoint benefit and gave higher complete-response gains in each background (Fig. 4e,f). For HeLa, for example, complete gain was 4.16% originally, 0.78% after whole attenuation and 4.92% after component control. The resulting partner gain remained slightly negative. The practical benefit is selective adjustment of the correction, rather than evidence that a strong new partner direction was learned. Full-precision gains and error components are reported for every configuration in Supplementary Table S37 and its source data.

The same rule left Jurkat's correction unchanged, slightly reduced the high-support K562 gain and left the low-support result unchanged. Across configurations, component control improved on the original in fifteen, tied in thirty-three and worsened in seven; these are descriptive counts with shared-query dependence. They show why retaining successful cases and adverse cases is necessary to evaluate the adjustment. Preserving endpoint contributions does not guarantee an external partner improvement.

Two checks clarify its use. Coefficients estimated separately from the measured repeats agreed closely in some backgrounds but differed markedly in HeLa. Dividing the same query identities into smaller panels also changed some gains, because each panel supports different endpoint-eliminated contrasts. The method therefore operates on a specified query panel, with coefficient fitting separated from query scoring. Supplementary Note 19 reports every repeat and panel result. The executable interface returns the baseline, original prediction, both correction components and the controlled prediction, allowing users to complete a partial screen while inspecting exactly what was adjusted.

We next asked whether target-screen measurements could determine how strongly to use an existing partner direction. In the Han K562 screen,[43] measured single responses supplied an additive baseline, and frozen twelve-parameter (SF12) and neural (V4) shared functions supplied corrections. Each function had been fitted in one of the three Shen backgrounds. Only its strength was estimated from Han calibration combinations; whole-pair splits excluded query outcomes. The full-screen extension retained all six configurations, with 20,359 eligible reconstructed pairs. Methods and Supplementary Note 22 define the shared functions and their source-to-target adaptation; Table S49 distinguishes this extension from the protocol's original common-identity primary analysis.

Target-side calibration improved the HeLa- and 293T-derived SF12 corrections relative to both applying them unchanged and removing them. Relative to removal, reconstructed partner error fell by 0.262% and 0.651%, respectively, yielding complete-response reductions of 0.187% and 0.465% over the additive baseline (Fig. 4g,h). The HeLa correction was initially excessive: attenuation removed substantial damage while retaining a small useful increment. The 293T direction was already beneficial and gained a smaller further improvement. Figure 4g reports reductions relative to the unchanged corrections, keeping that comparison separate from the net benefit over addition.

Both SF12 configurations improved over unchanged and removed corrections in all five pair partitions; their pooled net gains were positive in both reconstructed repeats and in the published-phenotype analysis. The other configurations did not show the same consistent benefit (Supplementary Fig. S5). These within-study checks support target-side calibration of particular existing directions. Here the added vector is already purely a partner correction, so the test complements Shen's comparison of whole and component attenuation. Tables S44–S49 give complete results, calibration-set sizes and analysis status.

### Readout shrinkage reduces adverse RNA corrections

The RNA extension asks whether training measurements can regulate partner correction across thousands of transcripts. We evaluated it on the matched CaRPool dataset used for model development.[16] Whole combinations were held out, and eleven four-pair rectangles compared predictions from the same fitted model. The baseline already included nonlinear composition and endpoint refinement. The additional kernel estimates corrections from endpoint-eliminated training responses using similarities between pairs' single-response profiles. Its held-out rectangle error tests the added prediction of partner contrasts.

The usefulness of a correction can differ between transcripts. A common amplitude may preserve a strong response change but also carry weak or inconsistent changes into other readouts. WitnessCell instead compares endpoint-eliminated responses in two disjoint halves of the training cells. A transcript with weaker agreement receives a smaller correction. Because the weights depend only on training measurements, the held-out combinations provide a separate test of whether this adjustment improves prediction.

Agreement between cell halves measures sampling consistency within the experiment. Shared guide or preparation effects can persist in both halves, so this weighting does not establish reproducibility across biological preparations. Its role is operational: it sets the amplitude applied to each transcript's prediction. A training-selected uniform shrinkage control tests whether transcript-specific weighting adds value beyond reducing the entire correction by one common factor.

The resulting predictor, R, reduced held-out rectangle error by 15.96% relative to its baseline and by 6.94% relative to uniform shrinkage (Fig. 5b,c). Thus, a single shrinkage factor did not account for the full improvement. An error-budget analysis separated transcripts with reduced error from those with increased error, determining the sign separately for each model. R preserved most of the unweighted correction's total positive error reduction while markedly lowering its total adverse increment (Fig. 5d). Supplementary Notes 6, 13 and 15 provide the full budgets, matched neural comparisons and individual-seed results.

The benefit was concentrated. Ten transcripts, including S100A9, S100A8, S100A12 and LYZ, accounted for over 90% of positive error reductions. Removing that fixed set or standardizing by training-condition variance eliminated the aggregate gain (Fig. 5e,f). Outside the set, shrinkage substantially reduced the harm caused by the unweighted correction, leaving the net gain close to zero and slightly negative. The useful RNA correction was therefore concentrated in a small subset of measured responses.

These sensitivity analyses distinguish the magnitude of the benefit from its breadth. Error on the original response scale gives greater weight to larger discrepancies, so correcting a few strongly changing transcripts can substantially improve that loss. Training-variance standardization changes the relative influence of readouts, whereas deletion asks how much benefit remains outside the selected set. The original-scale result demonstrates a useful correction for those responses; the two sensitivity results show why it should not be interpreted as improvement throughout the transcriptome.

Evaluations in Dixit and Combi-seq using fixed analysis procedures did not establish an additional partner gain, despite useful complete-response prediction in the Dixit baseline.[21,22] Together with the functional results, these outcomes show that adding a learner capable of changing partner contrasts does not ensure an improvement in every task. Supplementary Table S36 places the gains alongside each task's training counts, readouts and evaluation status. Training-based shrinkage improved the balance of useful and adverse corrections in the CaRPool development dataset. A supplementary replay applies the same four-policy comparison to all 265 eligible saved RNA candidate records, preserving their separate full-query projection loss and selection identities (Supplementary Note 20). The original eleven-rectangle result remains the primary RNA comparison.

### Further uses distinguish prediction from decision utility

We separately examined whether response models could identify lower-error queries and rank partners for a fixed transcriptional objective (Supplementary Fig. S1). Risk selection retained a subset with lower prediction error. PairPick executed the recommendation task, but its choices largely matched existing alternatives. Many detected intervention labels could not be matched to the original design table.[23] The recommendation result is consequently a retrospective demonstration on detected labels. These analyses distinguish three practical outcomes: predicting responses more accurately, identifying predictions with lower error and making better choices among candidates. ## Discussion

Combination measurements can improve a predictor by refining contributions shared across an intervention's partners or by learning how exchanging partners changes the response. WitnessCell makes this distinction testable within a working prediction method. Its endpoint residual improves complete responses while leaving defined partner contrasts unchanged. Explicit partner learners add the ability to change those contrasts, and the functional results show useful held-out gains beyond the same fitted baseline.

The distinction also guides how to use a learned correction. Complete-response improvement can coexist with partner damage because the same branch changes both components. A common shrinkage factor couples two decisions: how much endpoint refinement to keep and how much partner correction to apply. Component control separates them, retaining the original endpoint contribution while adjusting the partner part from internal held-combination predictions. Shen demonstrates the value of this separation: shrinking the entire correction can discard useful endpoint refinement. Han supplies a complementary result: target measurements can set a useful strength for some frozen shared functions. These operations extend the response-learning method by allowing its added predictions to be used selectively.

These results suggest a practical design sequence. Establish which query responses the measurements can distinguish; determine which response differences a proposed branch can change; fit and evaluate that increment against a baseline using the same observations; then adjust the relevant component when its strength is poorly matched to the target. The causal experiment establishes the information requirement within its specified mechanism family. The endpoint identity and component-preservation result specify model behavior exactly. Held-out biological measurements test whether the fitted additions and adjustments help.

This approach complements strong-baseline comparisons, Systema's analysis of systematic variation and Miller's metric calibration.[10,11,12] It asks what an added learner changes and makes that answer useful for controlling its output. The same questions apply to increasingly powerful representations, including scGPT, scFoundation and Geneformer.[24,25,26] Projection, kernel regression and matrix regularization supply established mathematical tools. Their role here is to identify a precise limitation of endpoint refinement, construct additional partner predictions and control those predictions without indiscriminately attenuating the endpoint contribution.

Functional partner gains support completion of substantially measured screens, while RNA gains concentrate in a small readout subset. Expanded K562 support improves the tested correction without establishing a universal condition-count threshold: coverage, layout, readout structure and noise change what can be learned. The retrospective control analysis reduces some harmful increments while retaining Jurkat's existing gain. Han adds modest benefits for two SF12 configurations under a target additive baseline. Obtaining a stronger prediction direction remains a separate learning task; repeat differences and query-panel dependence specify how control should be evaluated.

For researchers, the delivered workflow turns measured combinations into predictions, exposes the endpoint and partner contributions of an added learner, and supplies a targeted adjustment for a specified missing-combination panel. The original and controlled outputs remain available for comparison. Targeted RNA and multimodal screens provide further settings for these tests,[27,28,29] and selecting informative combinations could connect the same distinctions to experiment planning.[30,31] Within broader efforts toward predictive cellular models,[32,33] this provides a concrete progression from learning additional responses to checking and controlling how those responses enter a usable prediction.

## Methods

### Study design and implementation identities

The study reanalysed public perturbation measurements and used a specified synthetic causal system. No new biological samples were collected. The primary response method is the released WitnessCell transcriptomic core. The matched RNA partner predictor, functional growth model, analytic-risk predictor and empirical risk ranking are separately fitted implementations. WitnessCell–PairPick names the partner-selection application; its default response configuration is the archived R-G algorithm. PairPick-Stable and PairPick-TargetRank retain their separate identities. Model identifiers in the source archives are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Biological prediction splits assign unordered perturbation pairs, keeping associated cells, guides, orientations and repeated measurements together. Permitted singles remain available. Mixed activation/repression tasks additionally group mode variants by physical gene pair. Inner fitting reconstructs preprocessing, baseline and correction from the allowed observations. Development panels, procedure-transfer evaluations and source-transfer comparisons retain their original status. Repeated optimization seeds, overlapping folds, shared-source datasets and algebraic contrast dimensions are not counted as independent biological replications. The experiment records include unsuccessful registered analyses, subsequent development and locally frozen predictions. Supplementary Table S30 distinguishes development exposure, frozen procedures or predictions, and within-study versus between-study evaluation. Local separation of training and scoring is not independent third-party blinding.

### Data sources and response definitions

CRISPR repression and activation permit complementary genetic interventions.[34] Pooled single-cell assays link perturbation identities to molecular responses,[21,35,36,37] and genome-scale Perturb-seq provides broader gene-function context.[38] These assay precedents motivate the response definitions below; the benchmark sources are specified separately.

The released benchmark used scPerturBench-processed Norman, Wessels, Schmidt and Replogle_exp6 datasets, their Gene Ontology support filters and public split seeds 1–3.[9] Primary experimental sources include Norman GSE133344, Wessels GSE213957 and Replogle GSE146194; Schmidt retains the benchmark's processed labels with the primary T-cell study as provenance.[1,16,2,17] Benchmark and matched CaRPool analyses share the Wessels source and constitute different tasks, not independent source replication. The full benchmark contains 540 combination–seed and 114 unseen-single records.

RNA effects are condition means relative to the permitted control reference. Source preprocessing and gene axes are retained for each experiment. The matched CaRPool task has 55 pairs, fourteen singles, five outer folds, eleven same-fit evaluation rectangles and 4,995 non-target readouts. Its neural comparators retain their 5,020-gene model axis and use the same scoring mask. Growth phenotypes are repeat-specific Triple Sequencing measurements from GSE116198.[3] Independent checks use Dixit GSE90063/GSM2396860 and Combi-seq GSE174695.[21,22] The mixed-mode selection application uses GSE220974.[23]

### Single-response estimation and the released transcriptomic core

For a measured single $g$, the effect is $e_g=\mu_g-\mu_0$. Missing endpoints use a head combining the equal-condition mean of training singles, a ten-neighbour Jaccard-weighted Gene Ontology response and a self-response anchor. Leave-one-single feature construction excludes the endpoint being fitted. Coefficients use numerical ridge $10^{-8}$ and clipping to $[0,2]$. The additional on-target amplitude head uses generic self response and sign-matched tail and root-mean-square fingerprint features, relative ridge 0.2 and coefficient bounds $[-1.5,1.5]$.

For every outer training endpoint, nested checks remove that endpoint before fitting the base head and amplitude correction. Activation requires positive one-sided 95% lower bounds for both relative all-gene MSE gain and top-100 correlation gain; top-100 MSE is recorded but is not a third gate. Fewer than six usable endpoints retains the preceding head. Measured endpoints are unchanged. This amplitude extension is excluded from selection of the released combination hyperparameters.

With $e^{\mathrm{base}}$ and $e^{\mathrm{final}}$ denoting the preceding and final endpoint effects, the released predictor is

$$\widehat y_q=\mu_0+S_\alpha(e_g^{\mathrm{final}}+e_h^{\mathrm{final}})+\gamma K^{\mathrm{end}}_{QT}(K^{\mathrm{end}}_{TT}+\eta I)^{-1}R_T,$$

where $S_\alpha(z)=z/(1+\alpha|z|)$ acts coordinatewise, $K^{\mathrm{end}}_{TT}=X_TX_T^\top/2$ and $K^{\mathrm{end}}_{QT}=X_QX_T^\top/2$. Training residuals $R_T$ subtract $\mu_0+S_\alpha(e_g^{\mathrm{base}}+e_h^{\mathrm{base}})$. This conservative-residual/final-backbone convention reproduces the released implementation; endpoint versions coincide when their singles are measured.

Validation selects $\alpha\in\{0,.02,.05,.1,.2,.5\}$ and $\eta\in\{.01,.03,.1,.3,1\}$. For transferred correction $D$ and remaining validation error $E$, the amplitude is $\gamma=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\langle D,E\rangle/\|D\|_F^2$, with zero for zero correction energy. MSE ties favour smaller $\alpha$, then smaller $\eta$. The original use of validation combinations is retained and distinguished from later extensions selected entirely inside their outer training sets.

### Benchmark aggregation and response losses

The formal comparison reconstructs the released top-100 six-metric aggregation over all four datasets. Fifteen eligible comparator entries, including simple references, plus WitnessCell form the sixteen-entry track. The primary mean operation rank follows the benchmark's aggregation code; equal-dataset and two-panel aggregations are robustness summaries. Metric-specific ranks remain separate. Comparator-exclusion sensitivity reranks the archived correlation and five metric scores within each operation, using minimum ranks for ties, then reranks their six-rank mean and averages operation ranks. It changes only the retained method set; all 540 double records, scores and aggregation rules are fixed (Supplementary Table S31). For distributional metrics, the benchmark uses predicted means with training-control variation, rather than a fitted distribution of perturbed cell states.

The 540-record combination analysis uses the archived training-mean additive reference, which substitutes the equal-condition training-single mean for an unavailable endpoint. This self-contained weighted analysis uses that reference once. It is separate from the official `linearModel` scoring row. The released branch-level correspondence does not demonstrate identical historical score inputs: the archived double-only MSEs differ in 529 of 540 records (maximum absolute difference 0.0235). Both official rows remain unchanged; source and scoring-object distinctions are detailed in Supplementary Note 14. Later shared-endpoint addition, independently selected saturation and the nonlinear backbone selected jointly with the final residual are distinct comparators. ## Code availability

The Han reassessment archive contains frozen source weights, input transformations, compact measurements, per-fold coefficients and locked predictions. `code/replay_han.py --workdir /absolute/path/new_replay` reconstructs its frozen inference, target-side calibration and scoring; it does not retrain the base predictors. `code/revise_v36.py` generates the revised text, and `code/make_figures_v36.py` updates the method diagram and source–target labels from the retained figure sources. The earlier source records remain distinct from the full-screen analysis.

Source code, dependencies, numerical evidence and figure-generation scripts are supplied with this manuscript. The complete implementation and reproduction archive is included at `reproduction/grn_repo_v27.zip`, with its SHA-256 and task entry points. The new-input interfaces are `software/witnesscell_predict.py` for the `multihead_v14` core and `software/witnesscell_growth.py` for the functional procedure. Both fit from training observations without query outcomes. The core demonstration uses synthetic inputs; the functional demonstration reproduces predictions from the archived Jurkat training measurements. Native source hashes and interface checks document the correspondence to the scientific implementations. `partner_control/software/witnesscell_partner_control.py` fits the adjustment from supplied internal held-combination predictions and applies it without query outcomes. Its real-data example, all functional arrays, sufficient statistics for the supplementary RNA replay and complete evaluation scripts are included under `partner_control/`. This interface fits the control coefficient; the original RNA and functional interfaces fit the biological predictors.

The RNA R and mode-aware R-G implementations are available through their experiment entry points in the complete archive; PairPick uses the internal identifier `R_G`. Compact score replay, new-input fitting, neural-weight replay and raw-cell retraining have distinct documented scopes. The MIT licence covers only the five files explicitly named in `software/LICENSE-ADAPTER.txt`: the RNA and growth adapters, example generator and their two tests. It does not cover `partner_control/` or the original scientific modules. Their existing rights are retained; a unified author-approved redistribution licence remains to be supplied. Module-level licence information is provided in `RIGHTS_AND_RELEASE.md`.
