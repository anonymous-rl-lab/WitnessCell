# WitnessCell predicts combinatorial perturbation responses through endpoint and partner learning

## Abstract

Predicting unmeasured perturbation combinations requires informative experiments and models that exploit their response structure. WitnessCell separates nonlinear single-response composition, endpoint refinement and explicit partner correction, linking each component to a testable prediction capability. A controlled causal system establishes how joint interventions identify target responses that remain ambiguous under single interventions. The released predictor ranks first among sixteen eligible methods in a four-dataset benchmark and retains first place in its 540 double-perturbation records. In a matched RNA panel, a repeat-informed partner extension reduces contrast error by 15.96%, retaining concentrated positive gains while limiting adverse corrections. A frozen functional-prediction procedure reduces growth-response error by 6.43% across 617 Jurkat combinations. Risk selection and retrospective partner recommendation illustrate distinct research uses. WitnessCell turns partial combination screens into response predictions with experimentally assessable endpoint and partner contributions.

Combinatorial perturbation experiments reveal how cellular responses depend on interventions acting together. Their growing scale creates an opportunity for computational models to predict combinations that have not been measured, a central capability sought in virtual cells.[1,2] Single-cell profiles expose coordinated transcriptional changes, while growth measurements connect perturbations to a functional phenotype.[3,4] For a researcher with single-perturbation measurements and a partially observed combination screen, the practical objective is to obtain accurate responses for additional combinations and use those responses to decide which hypotheses deserve experimental attention. A useful method must therefore connect the measurements available to the particular response being predicted.

Existing approaches learn latent response representations, compose intervention embeddings, use biological graphs or adapt models pretrained on large expression collections.[5,6,7,8,9] These strategies address important aspects of generalization, including genes without measured single responses. Their performance also depends on the information supplied by the screen. Joint interventions may refine individual perturbation effects, reveal shared nonlinear response rules or expose a dependence on the identity of the partner. These sources of improvement have different scientific meanings even when they lower the same aggregate error. Recent benchmarks and strong linear baselines emphasize the need to identify the variation responsible for a claimed predictive advance.[10,11]

The distinction begins with causality. Two mechanisms can agree on all permitted single interventions while disagreeing on a double intervention. A suitable joint measurement can separate those explanations, making another combination response identifiable within a specified mechanism family. Conversely, increasing the number of observations of uninformative conditions can improve precision while leaving the relevant ambiguity intact. The target of interest may become identifiable before an entire regulatory network is determined. This target-centred perspective connects causal information to a concrete prediction task and separates an information limitation from an estimation limitation.

A second distinction concerns what the fitted model can change. Measurements of combinations often improve estimates of individual endpoint effects. Such refinement is valuable, especially when a single response is missing or noisy, but it does not necessarily teach how one perturbation responds differently to different partners. Endpoint-eliminating contrasts provide an operational test: they cancel arbitrary additive endpoint effects and retain response variation associated with exchanging partners. Classical estimability and prediction theory offer tools for making this separation explicit.[12,13] Biological measurements then determine whether a component that can represent a contrast actually predicts it accurately.

WitnessCell makes this separation part of the prediction method. Its released core combines measured or inferred single responses, nonlinear composition and regularized transfer from measured combinations. The endpoint-transfer correction has an exact restriction: it cannot change endpoint-eliminated contrasts. An explicit partner extension addresses that missing capacity, and repeated training measurements control how strongly the correction is delivered to each readout. This provides a testable construction for learning additional response structure beyond a strong baseline, rather than attributing every improvement to the same type of interaction.

The study connects three questions: which target responses the experiments can distinguish, which response components the predictor can learn, and which measured research outcomes improve. A controlled causal system addresses the first question. A multi-dataset benchmark establishes the released core's predictive performance, while RNA contrasts and growth measurements test additional partner structure. Functional growth prediction provides a direct biological application; selective prediction and partner recommendation illustrate further uses. The implementations share this response construction but retain their own parameters and evaluation populations. The scientific advance is to connect a usable combination predictor to an explicit account of the capability learned from joint measurements.

## Results

### WitnessCell constructs combination predictions from measured experimental support

WitnessCell takes control measurements, permitted single interventions, a training set of joint interventions and the identities of requested combinations. The response can be a vector of transcript abundances or a measured functional phenotype. Experimental support comprises the available intervention identities, their pattern of overlap and the measurements associated with them. The workflow first constructs a baseline response, then estimates a correction from the measured combinations. Model selection uses permitted training or validation observations, and evaluation excludes the complete identity of each query combination (Fig. 1a).

For transcriptomic prediction, a measured single effect is its condition mean relative to control. When an endpoint lacks a measured single, the released core combines the mean response of training singles, Gene Ontology neighbours and an on-target response anchor. A training-only amplitude extension refines the target coordinate when nested endpoint holdouts support the change. These ingredients allow the same core to address combinations with different levels of endpoint exposure. They also make the source of information explicit: a measured single supplies an observed effect, whereas an unmeasured endpoint uses a prediction from permitted response and annotation information.

The two single effects are combined through a coordinatewise saturation rule. A regularized endpoint-sharing kernel then transfers residuals from training combinations to the query. The final predictor therefore incorporates both the single-perturbation response and information learned from joint measurements. Its nonlinear composition and residual strength are selected using the declared validation procedure. This released configuration supplies the formal benchmark results below. The subsequent partner and functional implementations have their own fitted components and are evaluated as extensions of the response construction.

To determine which capability a correction adds, consider the unsigned endpoint-incidence matrix $X_Q$ for a collection of query pairs. Each row identifies the two interventions present in that pair. The projector

$$P_Q=I-X_QX_Q^{+}$$

removes responses expressible as arbitrary sums of endpoint contributions. For a four-pair rectangle, the corresponding contrast is $Y_{AB}-Y_{AD}-Y_{CB}+Y_{CD}$. It measures whether exchanging the same partners has different consequences in two backgrounds. This is a population-response comparison; the observations can come from different cells. Shared nonlinear composition can also generate such a contrast, so its interpretation is partner-sensitive response variation rather than a uniquely identified molecular interaction.

The released endpoint residual has the form $\delta_Q=X_Q\Theta$. Consequently, $P_Q\delta_Q=0$: this branch can improve complete responses but cannot add a new endpoint-eliminated contrast. The nonlinear baseline itself can contain partner-sensitive variation. This algebraic distinction motivates an explicit extension,

$$\widehat Y_{qj}=F_{\mathrm{core},qj}+w_jG_{qj},$$

where $G$ can change partner contrasts and $w_j$ controls the contribution for readout $j$. The RNA implementation uses a kernel built from single-response similarities; the growth implementation uses a symmetric residual matrix. Their usefulness is determined by held-out error relative to their own refitted baselines (Fig. 1b,c).

The construction provides a direct account of what a model has learned. Complete-response error assesses the prediction delivered to a researcher. Endpoint-eliminated error assesses a more specific additional capability. Readout shrinkage tests whether repeated training measurements support the proposed correction. For practical use, WitnessCell–PairPick projects predicted responses onto a specified transcriptional objective and selects among candidate partners. Its default evaluated response configuration retains the archival identifier R-G. Earlier PairPick-Stable and PairPick-TargetRank configurations remain separately identified, preserving the correspondence between each implementation and its evidence.

Configuration choice follows the user's inputs and objective before query outcomes are examined. Normalized RNA summaries and training/validation combinations support the released core, including its missing-single head. Measured endpoint responses, training sampling halves and an estimable partner design support the RNA R extension. Repeat-specific functional measurements use the growth residual model. A fixed transcriptional objective and mode-aware response predictions support PairPick. These are declared analysis paths, not a selector optimized on test performance. The accompanying new-input example executes the unchanged released core and reports predictions, their baseline and the fitted settings; the configuration guide identifies the separately evaluated extensions (Supplementary Table S29).

### Joint interventions resolve target ambiguities left by single perturbations

The information basis of combination prediction can be stated in terms of compatible mechanisms. For measured interventions $\mathcal E$, let $\mathcal M(\mathcal E)$ contain the mechanisms in a declared class that produce the same population observations. The compatible answers to query $q$ are

$$\mathcal T_q(\mathcal E)=\{\mu_m(q):m\in\mathcal M(\mathcal E)\}.$$

A query is identified within that class when these answers coincide. An informative joint intervention separates mechanisms whose disagreement affects the query. This formulation connects causal discrimination to prediction without requiring all internal parameters or network edges to become unique.

A controlled system illustrates the distinction. A binary source $X$ drives six mediators $A$ to $F$. The downstream response contains affine mediator effects and either a direct source contribution or one of three candidate gates, including $h_A=A\lor(B\land C)$. Under control and every permitted single mediator knockout, this gate equals the source. A direct effect and a gated effect can therefore produce the same observations. Joint knockout of $A$ and $B$ makes the gate zero while the source can remain active, revealing a distinction unavailable from those singles. The intervention semantics and candidate family are specified, while the active gate and response coefficients are hidden during estimation (Fig. 2a).

Two observation panels tested the consequence for an unmeasured target. Both contained control and seven single interventions. The informative panel added $AB,BC,DE,DF$; the comparison panel added $AD,BD,BE,CF$. These choices matched the number of conditions, every endpoint degree and the observation budget. The four query pairs $AC,AF,CE,EF$ were absent from both panels. Their target contrast was $\Delta=\mu_{AC}-\mu_{AF}-\mu_{CE}+\mu_{EF}$. Each condition contained 256 observations, giving 3,072 observations per panel (Fig. 2b).

In the comparison panel, four generating worlds produced identical training distributions but target values of $0,-0.5,0,-0.5$ (Fig. 2c). Any estimator $T$ using those observations has equal-world squared-error risk $\mathbb E[(T+0.25)^2]+1/16$, which is at least 0.0625. This bound holds for every fitting algorithm restricted to the panel. It identifies an ambiguity in the experiments themselves: more precise repetition of the same conditions preserves the competing explanations. The bound concerns the equal-world target risk rather than an error requirement for each world separately.

The informative panel exposed the relevant gate directions through $AB$ and $BC$. Applying the same candidate least-squares procedure to both panels across 1,000 paired noise seeds reduced target mean squared error from 0.065295 to 0.004674, a 92.84% decrease. The paired difference was −0.060620, with a 95% Monte Carlo interval of −0.061103 to −0.060137. Mean error across the four complete query responses fell from 0.018583 to 0.004189. Thus informative joint measurements supported predictions below the alternative panel's estimator-independent risk floor (Fig. 2d).

A generalized least-squares reference built from the measured $AB$ and $BC$ conditional contrasts achieved target error 0.005247. Its difference from the primary estimator was unresolved. This comparison attributes the large gain to the intervention information rather than to an exclusive advantage of one estimator. Candidate-family confidence sets likewise distinguished point estimation from target uncertainty. These calculations describe the stipulated observation model; the biological predictors do not inherit its coverage guarantee.

The gate-B world further distinguishes detecting an interaction from answering a target question. It generates interaction signals in the measured conditions, yet its requested contrast is zero. The appropriate inference is therefore that a particular target is zero despite detectable interactions elsewhere. By contrast, gates A and C imply the same nonzero target while remaining different internal explanations. These cases show why the relevant experimental distinction depends on the query: mechanisms can differ without requiring different predictions, and measurements can exhibit nonadditivity without resolving the response contrast of interest. The compatible-answer formulation retains exactly the disagreement that matters for that prediction.

The mechanism restriction is consequential. Allowing arbitrary simultaneous contributions of all three gates introduces a function that vanishes on the informative training panel but changes the query contrast. The target then becomes ambiguous again. The result establishes a precise scientific principle: joint interventions can resolve prediction-relevant causal ambiguity under declared assumptions, and their value depends on which mechanisms and targets must be distinguished. The following experiments ask how this information is converted into response accuracy in measured biological systems.

### The released predictor leads both the formal track and its double-perturbation subset

Predictive utility was evaluated in the released genetic-combination track of scPerturBench.[10] The comparison retained Norman, Wessels, Schmidt and Replogle_exp6, three public split seeds and the specified six-metric aggregation on the top 100 perturbation-affected genes.[3,14,15,16] Fifteen published eligible methods and WitnessCell contributed sixteen entries. The formal population comprises 654 operation–seed records: 540 double perturbations and 114 unseen singles. We preserved that ranking and decomposed the same saved operation scores by prediction target, without retraining or changing the aggregation rule.

WitnessCell ranked first in the full track, with mean operation rank 3.803, followed by Scouter at 5.411 and the released linear model at 5.725. In the double-only subset, WitnessCell retained first place with mean rank 3.515, followed by Scouter at 5.150; the linear model and GEARS had mean ranks 6.180 and 7.754 (Fig. 3a). Clustering repeated seed records by dataset and pair identity gave a WitnessCell–Scouter difference of −1.635, with a 95% bootstrap interval of −2.117 to −1.141. Corresponding differences were −2.665 against the linear model and −4.239 against GEARS, with intervals of −3.002 to −2.317 and −4.764 to −3.702 (Fig. 3c). These comparisons localize the aggregate lead to the principal combination-prediction task.

Endpoint exposure provided a further decomposition. WitnessCell ranked first by point estimate in each double-perturbation subgroup: neither endpoint seen, one seen and both seen, containing 43, 374 and 123 records, respectively (Fig. 3b). The subgroups share some underlying pair identities across split seeds and are not independent confirmations. In the 114 unseen-single records, WitnessCell ranked second at 5.167 behind the linear model at 3.570. The overall combination result therefore coexists with a distinct ordering for single-response generalization. Full sixteen-method tables, all reference contrasts and dataset-specific raw-loss estimates are provided in Supplementary Tables S25–S26 and their machine-readable sources.

The original metric profile remains informative. WitnessCell ranked first on correlation of perturbation-induced change, mean squared error, energy distance and symmetric Kullback–Leibler divergence, while its Wasserstein and common differential-expression positions were ninth and eighth (Supplementary Table S6). Distributional metrics use the benchmark's mean-based adapter with training-control variation; they evaluate that adapter and predicted mean. The full-track first-place position also persisted under equal-dataset weighting and the recorded two-gene-panel aggregation.

A separate absolute-error analysis of the 540 combination records found a 24.87% reduction in weighted MSE against the released training-mean additive reference, with loss ratio 0.7513 and pair-bootstrap interval 0.7031–0.8019. The archived linear-model combination branch returns this same reference prediction and is counted once in that analysis. The percentage is thus tied to its specific baseline and weighted loss; the sixteen-method ranking above supplies the comparison with the broader set of predictors.

These gains combine several useful operations. The single-response head estimates unavailable endpoints; nonlinear composition accounts for response saturation; and measured combinations refine shared endpoint contributions. After removing manipulated target coordinates, complete non-target MSE ratios against the shared-endpoint additive reference were 0.9049 in Norman and 0.6459 in Wessels, corresponding to reductions of 9.51% and 35.41%. An independently selected saturation comparator still left a 32.36% MSE reduction in Wessels. The advantage therefore extends to downstream readouts rather than being confined to the directly targeted genes.

In Wessels, increasing the retained residual library from one quarter to one half and all available training combinations increased MSE gain over the paired nonlinear backbone from 19.10% to 27.42% and 33.25%; weighted gains were 26.81%, 33.91% and 37.66% (Fig. 3d). The full-minus-quarter MSE gain was 14.15 percentage points, with a paired interval of 12.42–16.06. This comparison holds the backbone fixed and measures the return from additional combination support.

Structural attribution identifies what this residual transfer adds. The twelve released query graphs supplied 212 algebraic contrast dimensions. Comparing the final prediction with its own jointly selected nonlinear backbone changed projected-error ratios by at most $6.34\times10^{-8}$, within saved-array precision. The residual branch therefore behaved as its endpoint-space algebra predicts. Its strong complete-response performance supplies a practical baseline and motivates a precise next question: can a correction acting outside that space improve partner-sensitive responses on held-out combinations?

### An explicit partner component learns response variation beyond endpoint refinement

The RNA partner extension was evaluated on a matched CaRPool panel from the Wessels study.[14] Fourteen measured singles and 55 unordered combinations were assigned to five outer folds. All cells and repeated measurements of a combination followed its assignment. Eleven rectangles whose four predictions came from the same fitted model provided the primary contrast evaluation over 4,995 non-target readouts. The baseline $M_0$ included saturation and endpoint refinement and was refitted inside each permitted training subset. This panel informed development, so the evaluation tests held-out combinations within the development setting.

The additional component transfers residuals using symmetric products of single-response similarities. Training projection removes the freely fitted endpoint effects, and the query operator removes their corresponding continuation. The resulting kernel can change partner contrasts that the endpoint-sharing residual cannot. Its unit-weight prediction is $M_1=M_0+G$. This construction uses response similarity to share information across combinations, while evaluating the added component against a baseline already benefiting from combination measurements.

Repeated training measurements control the amplitude delivered to each transcript. After splitting permitted cells into disjoint halves and projecting away endpoint effects, the correction for readout $j$ is multiplied by the clipped cross-half agreement divided by average half energy. A readout with little reproducible projected structure receives a small correction. This rule changes predictions before scoring and uses no query response. The halves measure within-experiment sampling agreement and can share guide or preparation effects; their role is to adjust the correction rather than supply independent biological replication.

The unweighted partner extension reduced held-out rectangle error by 9.44% relative to $M_0$. Adding measurement-derived shrinkage, denoted $R$, increased the reduction to 15.96%, with complete-response error reduced by 9.28% (Fig. 4a,b). The shrinkage predictor improved contrast error in nine of eleven rectangles against $M_0$ (Fig. 4c) and in all eleven against both the unit-weight extension and a training-selected uniform shrinkage control. Relative to the latter control's error, the reduction was 6.94%. All five outer folds had positive aggregate gains, including one close to zero.

Matched GEARS and Scouter runs used the same outer combination identities and evaluation mask, each with three optimization seeds.[6,7] The $R$ extension reduced rectangle error by 10.81% against the GEARS seed-mean error and by 12.88% against Scouter. These comparisons aggregate seed-specific losses, whereas the later PairPick application averages prediction vectors before scoring. Inner-selection budgets also differed between the recorded RNA workflows. The results establish performance of those implementations on a common outer task, with optimization seeds characterizing fitting variation.

The response distribution reveals where the RNA benefit arises. Ten readouts accounted for 92.13% of the positive error reductions produced by $R$, while negative contributions remained in the primary loss. Under training-variance-standardized scoring, its gain was −0.118%; after removing the previously locked ten-readout set, the gain was −0.380% (Fig. 4e,f). The extension therefore captures useful raw-scale partner variation concentrated in a small response subset. This concentration is biologically and statistically relevant when interpreting an aggregate transcriptomic score.

A signed error budget explains the shrinkage benefit more directly (Fig. 4d). For each readout, we summed the reduction in squared error over all eleven rectangles, then separated positive reductions from adverse increments. Relative to the same total $M_0$ error, $M_1$ contributed 20.83% positive reduction and 11.39% adverse increment, giving 9.44% net improvement. For R these quantities were 17.64%, 1.68% and 15.96%. Thus R retained 84.71% of the positive reduction while reducing adverse increments by 85.25%. The calculation describes the same development panel; it shows how selective attenuation improves the net prediction rather than supplying an independent replication.

Outside the fixed ten-readout set, the adverse gain changed from −10.03% for $M_1$ to −0.38% for R. This connects the method's amplitude control to reduced damage in weakly supported readouts while preserving its strongest response changes. The fixed set includes S100A9, S100A8, S100A12 and LYZ; the archived four-corner responses show the changes being corrected without defining a newly validated biological program. Uniform shrinkage, 200 expression/variance-stratified weight permutations and matched-norm random increments remain complementary controls (Supplementary Notes 6 and 13).

Independent-source checks further separated baseline accuracy from partner transfer. In a Dixit RNA evaluation, the complete baseline improved over additivity by 23.43%, while the extra correction gave a 0.270% complete-response improvement and a −0.033% partner gain.[17] A frozen Combi-seq drug-program evaluation also lacked an additional partner improvement.[18] Together with the positive matched-panel result, these findings identify a concrete capability and its present range: explicit partner structure can improve prediction, but its transfer depends on the readout, measurement support and experimental context.

### Combination support enables prediction of functional growth responses

Growth phenotypes provide a complementary application with a direct functional readout and denser combination coverage. The analysis used repeat-specific measurements from the Horlbeck genetic-interaction study.[4] Each repeat had a baseline fitted from permitted single and double measurements. A symmetric matrix then modelled the mean residual shared across repeats. A signed rank-one matrix was the primary correction; a spectral-shrinkage matrix provided a more flexible secondary implementation.[19] Selection used training combinations, and both corrections were evaluated against the baseline refitted for the same observations.

For eighty K562 genes, low and high training budgets contained 421–422 and 2,317–2,318 pairs per fold. The 632 evaluated pairs stayed fixed across the three folds. Increasing support raised the rank-one correction's complete-response gain from 0.27% to 11.03%, while its partner gain rose from −0.033% to 11.68%. The high-budget spectral correction gave complete and partner gains of 11.39% and 10.67%. The baseline itself improved by 10.12% when supplied with additional observations. The data therefore supported separate benefits for the baseline and for the added partner structure (Fig. 5a,b).

These budgets change condition count, endpoint degree and layout coverage together. Their value lies in demonstrating that more extensive combination measurements can support a transferable residual prediction on fixed queries. The controlled mechanism experiment isolates layout information at equal budget; the growth experiment tests the return from expanding real experimental support. Considering both avoids attributing every biological data-volume effect to a single structural property of the design.

The procedure was frozen before evaluation in Jurkat, using 79 genes, 2,464 training pairs and 617 held-out pairs. Model coefficients were estimated from Jurkat training data. This tests portability of the procedure to another cellular environment within the same study. The primary rank-one correction reduced pooled growth-response error by 6.43% and endpoint-eliminated partner error by 6.18%. Complete gains were 4.45% and 8.88% in the two measured repeats. The spectral correction achieved gains of 8.16% and 5.90%, respectively (Fig. 5c,d). Removing each endpoint from scoring left complete rank-one gains between 4.95% and 7.10%.

Illustrative pairs show the practical meaning of the correction. For TTI2–PGK1, the mean baseline prediction was −0.274 against an observed response of −0.321; the correction moved it to −0.287. For TTI2–OGFOD1, the baseline was −0.069 against −0.036, and the correction moved it to −0.058. The shared TTI2 endpoint receives corrections in opposite directions depending on its partner (Fig. 5e). These descriptively selected examples explain a prediction already evaluated across all pairs; they do not establish the molecular mechanism behind the response.

For a user of a partial growth screen, the workflow is complete: provide measured singles and combinations, fit the baseline and residual matrix, and obtain functional predictions for remaining pairs. The held-out measurements assess the utility of that output. This application gives the common response construction a functional interpretation independent of any RNA-based recommendation. It shows that a modest structured correction, when supported by sufficient measured combinations, can improve an experimentally meaningful phenotype.

The signed low-rank structure permits buffering and aggravating corrections through a shared statistical representation. Its interpretation is a pattern of functional residuals across partners, with the global evaluation establishing whether that pattern generalizes. The two repeats test whether gains persist across repeated phenotype measurements, and the second cell environment tests the portability of the fitting procedure. These different comparisons connect a compact computational construction to a useful functional outcome without requiring the matrix factors to be assigned a molecular identity.

### Risk selection and PairPick connect predictions to research decisions

Two distinct uses connect response prediction to subsequent research operations. Risk ranking asks which predictions are more dependable within a specified evaluation setting. Partner selection asks which candidate best matches an explicit objective. Their validation uses different outputs and denominators, so each application is evaluated through its own completed procedure.

An analytic-risk implementation was calibrated on development pairs and evaluated on 213 records from 33 pair identities absent from calibration. A fixed threshold retained 110 records at 49.48% pair-balanced coverage. Accepted predictions had an MSE ratio of 0.5343 relative to all predictions, with a pair-cluster bootstrap interval of 0.3646–0.7591 (Fig. 6b). A separate empirical ranking of the released predictor showed an 8.27% improvement over an endpoint-exposure control at 50% coverage in Wessels; the corresponding Norman difference was unresolved. These analyses support selective use in the tested settings and distinguish empirical risk ranking from a general error guarantee.

WitnessCell–PairPick provides a different operation: a user specifies an anchor intervention and a transcriptional objective, and predicted candidate responses are scored against that objective (Fig. 6a). The default evaluated R-G configuration supplies the response predictions. In the CRISPRai source GSE220974, the comparison included 28 fixed-anchor tasks over 49 detected mode-specific double labels, with activation and repression distinguished.[20] The five primary methods shared candidate pools and physical-pair holdouts. GEARS and Scouter each completed five folds and three seeds, with their prediction means scored as the ensemble.

PairPick (R-G) had mean regret 0.006197 and selected the observed best candidate in 19 of 28 tasks. Standard linear prediction, Scouter, GEARS and single-response addition had regrets of 0.006591, 0.006623, 0.010258 and 0.012220, with 18, 17, 18 and 18 hits. Standard linear prediction had the lowest RNA MSE, and GEARS the lowest partner MSE. An archived PRESAGE comparator made the same 28 choices as R-G. Thus the application demonstrates useful recommendations and the distinction between prediction and choice metrics, with limited evidence for superiority across all comparators (Fig. 6c,d).

Figure 6c displays all 28 tasks, ordered by anchor, mode and fold rather than outcome. PairPick improved achieved utility in one task and tied in 27 against linear prediction; it improved two and tied in 26 against Scouter. Against GEARS it had six wins, seventeen ties and five losses. These complete task distributions explain why mean regret and hit count give different impressions and why a modest prediction change often leaves the selected candidate unchanged. The eight-anchor intervals retain that dependence (Fig. 6d).

Construct identity limits biological interpretation of this application. We obtained the original CRISPRai design spreadsheet and compared all 49 detected labels with its K562 double-guide table. Fifteen labels directly matched designed gene-mode pairs, one corresponded only after collapsing an enhancer-specific design, and 33 were not listed as designed pairs. A gene-mode match itself does not verify the guide combination in each cell. The entire cohort and its original predictions are retained as a retrospective detected-label demonstration, rather than an independent confirmation of intended combinatorial interventions (Supplementary Table S28). Its operational output is a ranked candidate within a specified transcriptional objective; the K562/Jurkat experiments provide the separately measured functional application.

## Discussion

WitnessCell turns partial combination screens into response predictions whose learned components can be tested separately. The released core is competitive across the formal benchmark and leads its double-perturbation subset. The explicit partner extension addresses a capacity that the endpoint-transfer branch mathematically lacks. This combination of construction, attribution and held-out evaluation makes the contribution identifiable: joint measurements improve a useful predictor, and an additional model component is evaluated for the specific response variation it can change.

The causal analysis supplies the information principle behind this approach. Joint interventions can distinguish mechanisms that agree on permitted singles while disagreeing on a target combination. Equal sample counts and endpoint coverage therefore need not imply equal prediction limits. Identification remains conditional on the declared mechanism family, and a strong estimator can exploit the same informative layout. This result separates a limitation in the observations from a limitation in how a predictor uses them, while permitting the scientific target to be a response rather than a complete network.

The RNA analysis makes the role of measurement-informed attenuation concrete. R retains most of the favorable readout-level error reduction while substantially suppressing adverse corrections. Its gain is concentrated, and independent-source RNA/program evaluations do not establish general partner transfer. The functional results identify a complementary setting: denser measured combination support enables a compact residual model to improve growth prediction, and a frozen procedure retains value after fitting in Jurkat. These observations suggest that transferable partner learning depends on the response and available support as well as on representational capacity.

For experimental users, the direct application is to extend a partially observed screen with predictions for complete held-out combinations. The functional branch predicts a measured growth phenotype. PairPick makes a transcriptional objective explicit and reports the utility of a chosen partner; its detected-label demonstration has the construct-identity limitations established by the original design audit. Risk ranking is evaluated separately. Choosing the implementation from the input assay and intended output, and exposing the fitted settings, makes these distinct capabilities usable without requiring a single parameter set across readouts.

The evidence also suggests a reusable evaluation practice: report complete-response accuracy, identify which response space a correction changes, test that component on held-out combinations, and assess any claimed decision benefit at its own endpoint. Such comparisons reveal when endpoint refinement is already useful, when an additional partner component earns its complexity, and when a prediction improvement leaves the research decision unchanged. Future acquisition methods could target the measurements that resolve relevant ambiguities.[21,22] The retained one-measurement tests did not confirm an outer-target improvement, so adaptive design remains a separate research direction. The present advance is a predictor and response decomposition that connect existing joint measurements to assessable transcriptional and functional capabilities.

## Methods

### Study design and implementation identities

The study reanalysed public perturbation measurements and used a specified synthetic causal system. No new biological samples were collected. The primary response method is the released WitnessCell transcriptomic core. The matched RNA partner predictor, functional growth model, analytic-risk predictor and empirical risk ranking are separately fitted implementations. WitnessCell–PairPick names the partner-selection application; its default response configuration is the archived R-G algorithm. PairPick-Stable and PairPick-TargetRank retain their separate identities. Naming changes do not alter fitted predictions, losses or archived model identifiers.

Biological prediction splits assign unordered perturbation pairs, keeping associated cells, guides, orientations and repeated measurements together. Permitted singles remain available. Mixed activation/repression tasks additionally group mode variants by physical gene pair. Inner fitting reconstructs preprocessing, baseline and correction from the allowed observations. Development panels, procedure-transfer evaluations and source-transfer comparisons retain their original status. Repeated optimization seeds, overlapping folds, shared-source datasets and algebraic contrast dimensions are not counted as independent biological replications. The archive preserves original failures, subsequent development and local prediction freezes.

### Data sources and response definitions

The released benchmark used scPerturBench-processed Norman, Wessels, Schmidt and Replogle_exp6 datasets, their Gene Ontology support filters and public split seeds 1–3.[10] Primary experimental sources include Norman GSE133344, Wessels GSE213957 and Replogle GSE146194; Schmidt retains the benchmark's processed labels with the primary T-cell study as provenance.[3,14,15,16] Benchmark and matched CaRPool analyses share the Wessels source and constitute different tasks, not independent source replication. The full benchmark contains 540 combination–seed and 114 unseen-single records.

RNA effects are condition means relative to the permitted control reference. Source preprocessing and gene axes are retained for each experiment. The matched CaRPool task has 55 pairs, fourteen singles, five outer folds, eleven same-fit evaluation rectangles and 4,995 non-target readouts. Its neural comparators retain their 5,020-gene model axis and use the same scoring mask. Growth phenotypes are repeat-specific Triple Sequencing measurements from GSE116198.[4] Independent checks use Dixit GSE90063/GSM2396860 and Combi-seq GSE174695.[17,18] The mixed-mode selection application uses GSE220974.[20]

### Single-response estimation and the released transcriptomic core

For a measured single $g$, the effect is $e_g=\mu_g-\mu_0$. Missing endpoints use a head combining the equal-condition mean of training singles, a ten-neighbour Jaccard-weighted Gene Ontology response and a self-response anchor. Leave-one-single feature construction excludes the endpoint being fitted. Coefficients use numerical ridge $10^{-8}$ and clipping to $[0,2]$. The additional on-target amplitude head uses generic self response and sign-matched tail and root-mean-square fingerprint features, relative ridge 0.2 and coefficient bounds $[-1.5,1.5]$.

For every outer training endpoint, nested checks remove that endpoint before fitting the base head and amplitude correction. Activation requires positive one-sided 95% lower bounds for both relative all-gene MSE gain and top-100 correlation gain; top-100 MSE is recorded but is not a third gate. $$K_{(g,h),(u,v)}=\tfrac12(L_{gu}L_{hv}+L_{gv}L_{hu}).$$

With $P_T=I-X_TX_T^+$ and $A_Q=X_QX_T^+$, the training and query blocks are $P_TK_{TT}P_T$ and $(K_{QT}-A_QK_{TT})P_T$. Both are divided by the projected training trace per pair. Three inner folds select ridge from $\{.1,1,10\}$ by available complete-rectangle loss, using complete-response loss when no rectangle exists; ties favour stronger regularization. Every inner fit rebuilds $M_0$ from its permitted observations.

Disjoint training cell halves supply projected responses $Z_1=P_TY_T^{(1)}$ and $Z_2=P_TY_T^{(2)}$. The readout multiplier is

$$w_j=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\frac{Z_{1,\cdot j}^{\top}Z_{2,\cdot j}}{(\|Z_{1,\cdot j}\|^2+\|Z_{2,\cdot j}\|^2)/2},\qquad \widehat Y_R=M_0+G\operatorname{diag}(w).$$

### Risk ranking and selective prediction

The analytic implementation uses $y=X\beta+u+\varepsilon$, with $\Sigma=\operatorname{Cov}(u+\varepsilon)$, $k_t=\operatorname{Cov}(u+\varepsilon,u_t)$ and $k_{tt}=\operatorname{Var}(u_t)$. Under the unbiasedness constraint $X^\top a=t$, the minimum-risk linear predictor has coefficient

$$a_t=\Sigma^{-1}k_t+\Sigma^{-1}X(X^\top\Sigma^{-1}X)^{-1}(t-X^\top\Sigma^{-1}k_t),$$

$$W_t=k_{tt}-k_t^\top\Sigma^{-1}k_t+\widetilde t^\top(X^\top\Sigma^{-1}X)^{-1}\widetilde t,\qquad\widetilde t=t-X^\top\Sigma^{-1}k_t.$$
