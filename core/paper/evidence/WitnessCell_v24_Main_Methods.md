# WitnessCell predicts combinatorial perturbation responses using experimental support

## Abstract

Predicting unmeasured perturbation combinations requires both informative experiments and models that use their information effectively. WitnessCell combines single-perturbation responses with measured combinations to predict transcriptional and functional responses, separating endpoint refinement from partner-dependent corrections. A controlled causal system shows how joint interventions resolve target ambiguities that persist under single interventions. In the released genetic-combination benchmark, the response predictor ranks first among sixteen eligible entries across four datasets under the specified six-metric aggregation. An explicit partner extension reduces held-out contrast error by 15.96% in a matched RNA panel, with gains concentrated in a small readout subset. A frozen functional-prediction procedure reduces growth-response error by 6.43% across 617 Jurkat combinations. Risk-based selection and WitnessCell–PairPick illustrate how predictions can support subsequent research decisions. These results connect experimental information, distinguishable response components and measured predictive utility within a common computational approach.

Combinatorial perturbation experiments reveal how cellular responses depend on interventions acting together. Their growing scale creates an opportunity for computational models to predict combinations that have not been measured, a central capability sought in virtual cells.[1,2] Single-cell profiles expose coordinated transcriptional changes, while growth measurements connect perturbations to a functional phenotype.[3,4] For a researcher with single-perturbation measurements and a partially observed combination screen, the practical objective is to obtain accurate responses for additional combinations and use those responses to decide which hypotheses deserve experimental attention. A useful method must therefore connect the measurements available to the particular response being predicted.

Existing approaches learn latent response representations, compose intervention embeddings, use biological graphs or adapt models pretrained on large expression collections.[5,6,7,8,9] These strategies address important aspects of generalization, including genes without measured single responses. Their performance also depends on the information supplied by the screen. Joint interventions may refine individual perturbation effects, reveal shared nonlinear response rules or expose a dependence on the identity of the partner. These sources of improvement have different scientific meanings even when they lower the same aggregate error. Recent benchmarks and strong linear baselines emphasize the need to identify the variation responsible for a claimed predictive advance.[10,11]

The distinction begins with causality. Two mechanisms can agree on all permitted single interventions while disagreeing on a double intervention. A suitable joint measurement can separate those explanations, making another combination response identifiable within a specified mechanism family. Conversely, increasing the number of observations of uninformative conditions can improve precision while leaving the relevant ambiguity intact. The target of interest may become identifiable before an entire regulatory network is determined. This target-centred perspective connects causal information to a concrete prediction task and separates an information limitation from an estimation limitation.

A second distinction concerns what the fitted model can change. Measurements of combinations often improve estimates of individual endpoint effects. Such refinement is valuable, especially when a single response is missing or noisy, but it does not necessarily teach how one perturbation responds differently to different partners. Endpoint-eliminating contrasts provide an operational test: they cancel arbitrary additive endpoint effects and retain response variation associated with exchanging partners. Classical estimability and prediction theory offer tools for making this separation explicit.[12,13] Biological measurements then determine whether a component that can represent a contrast actually predicts it accurately.

These distinctions matter for interpreting a partial screen. A researcher interested in the total transcriptional response benefits from improvements in any component. A researcher comparing alternative partners needs the differences between those combinations to be preserved. A researcher planning a functional follow-up additionally needs a response that is relevant to the measured phenotype. The same aggregate prediction score cannot answer all three questions. Linking each scientific objective to a response component and a held-out evaluation creates a common basis for comparing methods while retaining the outcome that matters for the intended use.

WitnessCell addresses these questions through a response predictor and explicitly defined extensions. Its released transcriptomic core combines measured or inferred single responses, nonlinear composition and regularized transfer from measured combinations. A partner extension learns residual structure outside the free endpoint space and adjusts its contribution using repeated training measurements. A related residual construction predicts growth phenotypes. These implementations share the principle of separating a well-supported baseline from an additional response component whose value is tested on held-out combinations. Their fitted parameters and evaluation populations remain distinct.

The study evaluates this approach at complementary levels. A controlled causal system establishes the target-relevant information supplied by joint interventions. A released benchmark tests the competitiveness of the main response predictor. RNA contrasts and growth measurements assess what additional response structure can be learned, and risk ranking and partner selection illustrate how the predictions can be used. Together, these analyses connect methodological construction to scientific interpretation and practical use. The central advance is a way to exploit combination measurements while identifying the response capability supported by each gain, so that predictive accuracy, partner dependence and decision utility can each be assessed on their own evidence.

## Results

### WitnessCell constructs combination predictions from measured experimental support

WitnessCell takes control measurements, permitted single interventions, a training set of joint interventions and the identities of requested combinations. The response can be a vector of transcript abundances or a measured functional phenotype. Experimental support comprises the available intervention identities, their pattern of overlap and the measurements associated with them. The workflow first constructs a baseline response, then estimates a correction from the measured combinations. Model selection uses permitted training or validation observations, and evaluation excludes the complete identity of each query combination.

For transcriptomic prediction, a measured single effect is its condition mean relative to control. When an endpoint lacks a measured single, the released core combines the mean response of training singles, Gene Ontology neighbours and an on-target response anchor. A training-only amplitude extension refines the target coordinate when nested endpoint holdouts support the change. These ingredients allow the same core to address combinations with different levels of endpoint exposure. They also make the source of information explicit: a measured single supplies an observed effect, whereas an unmeasured endpoint uses a prediction from permitted response and annotation information.

The two single effects are combined through a coordinatewise saturation rule. A regularized endpoint-sharing kernel then transfers residuals from training combinations to the query. The final predictor therefore incorporates both the single-perturbation response and information learned from joint measurements. Its nonlinear composition and residual strength are selected using the declared validation procedure. This released configuration supplies the formal benchmark results below. The subsequent partner and functional implementations have their own fitted components and are evaluated as extensions of the response construction.

To determine which capability a correction adds, consider the unsigned endpoint-incidence matrix $X_Q$ for a collection of query pairs. Each row identifies the two interventions present in that pair. The projector

$$P_Q=I-X_QX_Q^{+}$$

removes responses expressible as arbitrary sums of endpoint contributions. For a four-pair rectangle, the corresponding contrast is $Y_{AB}-Y_{AD}-Y_{CB}+Y_{CD}$. It measures whether exchanging the same partners has different consequences in two backgrounds. This is a population-response comparison; the observations can come from different cells. Shared nonlinear composition can also generate such a contrast, so its interpretation is partner-sensitive response variation rather than a uniquely identified molecular interaction.

The released endpoint residual has the form $\delta_Q=X_Q\Theta$. Consequently, $P_Q\delta_Q=0$: this branch can improve complete responses but cannot add a new endpoint-eliminated contrast. The nonlinear baseline itself can contain partner-sensitive variation. This algebraic distinction motivates an explicit extension,

$$\widehat Y_{qj}=F_{\mathrm{core},qj}+w_jG_{qj},$$

where $G$ can change partner contrasts and $w_j$ controls the contribution for readout $j$. The RNA implementation uses a kernel built from single-response similarities; the growth implementation uses a symmetric residual matrix. Their usefulness is determined by held-out error relative to their own refitted baselines.

The construction provides a direct account of what a model has learned. Complete-response error assesses the prediction delivered to a researcher. Endpoint-eliminated error assesses a more specific additional capability. Readout shrinkage tests whether repeated training measurements support the proposed correction. For practical use, WitnessCell–PairPick projects predicted responses onto a specified transcriptional objective and selects among candidate partners. Its default evaluated response configuration retains the archival identifier R-G. Earlier PairPick-Stable and PairPick-TargetRank configurations remain separately identified, preserving the correspondence between each implementation and its evidence.

The separation also determines how the models are fitted. The partner kernel removes endpoint directions using the training design, and its continuation to new combinations depends on their identities and permitted single responses. It does not fit the query outcomes to define the correction. Evaluation then applies the query projector to the completed predictions and observed responses. Thus training constructs a transferable component, while the held-out contrast measures its performance. This distinction allows a model to be assessed for partner learning without turning the evaluation contrast into an additional training signal.

### Joint interventions resolve target ambiguities left by single perturbations

The information basis of combination prediction can be stated in terms of compatible mechanisms. For measured interventions $\mathcal E$, let $\mathcal M(\mathcal E)$ contain the mechanisms in a declared class that produce the same population observations. The compatible answers to query $q$ are

$$\mathcal T_q(\mathcal E)=\{\mu_m(q):m\in\mathcal M(\mathcal E)\}.$$

A query is identified within that class when these answers coincide. An informative joint intervention separates mechanisms whose disagreement affects the query. This formulation connects causal discrimination to prediction without requiring all internal parameters or network edges to become unique.

A controlled system illustrates the distinction. A binary source $X$ drives six mediators $A$ to $F$. The downstream response contains affine mediator effects and either a direct source contribution or one of three candidate gates, including $h_A=A\lor(B\land C)$. Under control and every permitted single mediator knockout, this gate equals the source. A direct effect and a gated effect can therefore produce the same observations. Joint knockout of $A$ and $B$ makes the gate zero while the source can remain active, revealing a distinction unavailable from those singles. The intervention semantics and candidate family are specified, while the active gate and response coefficients are hidden during estimation.

Two observation panels tested the consequence for an unmeasured target. Both contained control and seven single interventions. The informative panel added $AB,BC,DE,DF$; the comparison panel added $AD,BD,BE,CF$. These choices matched the number of conditions, every endpoint degree and the observation budget. The four query pairs $AC,AF,CE,EF$ were absent from both panels. Their target contrast was $\Delta=\mu_{AC}-\mu_{AF}-\mu_{CE}+\mu_{EF}$. Each condition contained 256 observations, giving 3,072 observations per panel.

In the comparison panel, four generating worlds produced identical training distributions but target values of $0,-0.5,0,-0.5$. Any estimator $T$ using those observations has equal-world squared-error risk $\mathbb E[(T+0.25)^2]+1/16$, which is at least 0.0625. This bound holds for every fitting algorithm restricted to the panel. It identifies an ambiguity in the experiments themselves: more precise repetition of the same conditions preserves the competing explanations. The bound concerns the equal-world target risk rather than an error requirement for each world separately.

The informative panel exposed the relevant gate directions through $AB$ and $BC$. Applying the same candidate least-squares procedure to both panels across 1,000 paired noise seeds reduced target mean squared error from 0.065295 to 0.004674, a 92.84% decrease. The paired difference was −0.060620, with a 95% Monte Carlo interval of −0.061103 to −0.060137. Mean error across the four complete query responses fell from 0.018583 to 0.004189. Thus informative joint measurements supported predictions below the alternative panel's estimator-independent risk floor.

A generalized least-squares reference built from the measured $AB$ and $BC$ conditional contrasts achieved target error 0.005247. Its difference from the primary estimator was unresolved. This comparison attributes the large gain to the intervention information rather than to an exclusive advantage of one estimator. Candidate-family confidence sets likewise distinguished point estimation from target uncertainty. These calculations describe the stipulated observation model; the biological predictors do not inherit its coverage guarantee.

The gate-B world further distinguishes detecting an interaction from answering a target question. It generates interaction signals in the measured conditions, yet its requested contrast is zero. The appropriate inference is therefore that a particular target is zero despite detectable interactions elsewhere. By contrast, gates A and C imply the same nonzero target while remaining different internal explanations. These cases show why the relevant experimental distinction depends on the query: mechanisms can differ without requiring different predictions, and measurements can exhibit nonadditivity without resolving the response contrast of interest. The compatible-answer formulation retains exactly the disagreement that matters for that prediction.

The mechanism restriction is consequential. Allowing arbitrary simultaneous contributions of all three gates introduces a function that vanishes on the informative training panel but changes the query contrast. The target then becomes ambiguous again. The result establishes a precise scientific principle: joint interventions can resolve prediction-relevant causal ambiguity under declared assumptions, and their value depends on which mechanisms and targets must be distinguished. The following experiments ask how this information is converted into response accuracy in measured biological systems.

### The released response predictor leads the formal combination benchmark

Predictive utility was evaluated using the released genetic-combination track of scPerturBench.[10] The comparison retained its four datasets, Norman, Wessels, Schmidt and Replogle_exp6, three public split seeds and specified aggregation over six metrics on the top 100 perturbation-affected genes.[3,14,15,16] Fifteen published eligible methods and WitnessCell contributed sixteen entries. The broader benchmark includes additional methods and tasks with different eligibility; the reported ranking refers to this defined track. Its 654 operation–seed records comprise 540 combination records and 114 unseen-single records.

WitnessCell ranked first with mean operation rank 3.803, followed by Scouter at 5.411 and the released linear model at 5.725. GEARS and CPA had mean ranks of 7.651 and 9.251. The first-place position persisted when datasets were given equal weight and under the recorded aggregation across both gene panels. These checks reduce dependence on the number of operations contributed by any one dataset. They provide broad evidence for the released predictor, whose parameters and evaluation recipe were fixed independently of the later partner-selection application.

The metric profile clarifies the nature of the advantage. WitnessCell ranked first on correlation of perturbation-induced change, mean squared error, energy distance and symmetric Kullback–Leibler divergence, while its Wasserstein and common differential-expression rankings were ninth and eighth. Its strongest performance therefore concerns several complementary aspects of the delivered response, with remaining weaknesses in other aspects. Distributional metrics were generated through the benchmark's mean-based adapter using training-control variation. Their scores assess that adapter and predicted mean rather than a learned distribution of cellular responses.

An absolute-error analysis isolated the 540 held-out combination–seed records. Against the released training-mean additive reference, WitnessCell reduced weighted mean squared error by 24.87%, corresponding to a loss ratio of 0.7513 with a pair-bootstrap interval of 0.7031–0.8019. The archived linear-model combination branch returned the same reference prediction and was counted once in this analysis. Accordingly, the 24.87% estimate describes this specific baseline comparison; it is separate from the multi-method aggregate ranking. Repeated seed records were carried together when resampling pair identities.

These gains reflect several useful operations. The single-response head handles missing endpoints, saturation accommodates departures from direct additivity, and measured combinations refine shared endpoint effects. Later controls removed perturbed target coordinates to examine whether improvements extended to other transcripts. Against a shared-endpoint additive reference, complete non-target MSE ratios were 0.9049 in Norman and 0.6459 in Wessels, corresponding to reductions of 9.51% and 35.41%. In Wessels, an independently selected saturation comparator still left a 32.36% MSE reduction. These controls help separate improvement in downstream response prediction from the direct prediction of perturbed coordinates.

The breadth of the benchmark and the specificity of these controls answer complementary questions. The common benchmark establishes how the released output compares with other eligible methods under a shared evaluation recipe. The paired controls isolate information and modelling changes inside that output. For example, comparing with direct addition tests a broader combination benefit, whereas comparison with the jointly selected backbone isolates the endpoint residual itself. Keeping these comparisons separate makes a large benchmark gain interpretable without assigning it to a component that was introduced in a later implementation.

Experimental support also affected the benefit obtained from measured combinations. In Wessels, quarter, half and full residual libraries gave complete MSE reductions of 19.10%, 27.42% and 33.25% relative to the fixed backbone. All nine recorded MSE trajectories improved at both increments. The full-minus-quarter gain was 14.15 percentage points, with a pair-bootstrap interval of 12.42–16.06. This comparison holds the prediction backbone fixed and changes the measured residual support available to it. It establishes a useful return from additional combinations for the specified response endpoint.

Structural attribution gives a different, complementary result. Across the twelve released splits, the query graphs supplied 212 algebraic contrast dimensions. Comparing the final endpoint-refined prediction with its own jointly selected nonlinear backbone changed projected error ratios by at most $6.34\times10^{-8}$, within saved-array precision. The residual branch's observed behavior thus matched its algebraic restriction. This does not diminish the complete-response gains; it identifies the capability responsible for them and establishes the appropriate baseline for a new partner component.

The benchmark therefore supports a substantial predictive contribution while motivating a more discriminating next question. Combination measurements already improve the released response predictor, including downstream transcripts. To establish learning of additional partner-dependent variation, a correction must both act outside the endpoint space and improve that component on unseen combinations. The matched RNA and functional experiments were designed to evaluate that additional capability directly.

### An explicit partner component learns response variation beyond endpoint refinement

The RNA partner extension was evaluated on a matched CaRPool panel from the Wessels study.[14] Fourteen measured singles and 55 unordered combinations were assigned to five outer folds. All cells and repeated measurements of a combination followed its assignment. Eleven rectangles whose four predictions came from the same fitted model provided the primary contrast evaluation over 4,995 non-target readouts. The baseline $M_0$ included saturation and endpoint refinement and was refitted inside each permitted training subset. This panel informed development, so the evaluation tests held-out combinations within the development setting.

The additional component transfers residuals using symmetric products of single-response similarities. Training projection removes the freely fitted endpoint effects, and the query operator removes their corresponding continuation. The resulting kernel can change partner contrasts that the endpoint-sharing residual cannot. Its unit-weight prediction is $M_1=M_0+G$. This construction uses response similarity to share information across combinations, while evaluating the added component against a baseline already benefiting from combination measurements.

Repeated training measurements control the amplitude delivered to each transcript. After splitting permitted cells into disjoint halves and projecting away endpoint effects, the correction for readout $j$ is multiplied by the clipped cross-half agreement divided by average half energy. A readout with little reproducible projected structure receives a small correction. This rule changes predictions before scoring and uses no query response. The halves measure within-experiment sampling agreement and can share guide or preparation effects; their role is to adjust the correction rather than supply independent biological replication.

The unweighted partner extension reduced held-out rectangle error by 9.44% relative to $M_0$. Adding measurement-derived shrinkage, denoted $R$, increased the reduction to 15.96%, with complete-response error reduced by 9.28%. The shrinkage predictor improved contrast error in nine of eleven rectangles against $M_0$ and in all eleven against both the unit-weight extension and a training-selected uniform shrinkage control. Relative to the latter control's error, the reduction was 6.94%. All five outer folds had positive aggregate gains, including one close to zero.

Matched GEARS and Scouter runs used the same outer combination identities and evaluation mask, each with three optimization seeds.[6,7] The $R$ extension reduced rectangle error by 10.81% against the GEARS seed-mean error and by 12.88% against Scouter. These comparisons aggregate seed-specific losses, whereas the later PairPick application averages prediction vectors before scoring. Inner-selection budgets also differed between the recorded RNA workflows. The results establish performance of those implementations on a common outer task, with optimization seeds characterizing fitting variation.

The response distribution reveals where the RNA benefit arises. Ten readouts accounted for 92.13% of the positive error reductions produced by $R$, while negative contributions remained in the primary loss. Under training-variance-standardized scoring, its gain was −0.118%; after removing the previously locked ten-readout set, the gain was −0.380%. The extension therefore captures useful raw-scale partner variation concentrated in a small response subset. This concentration is biologically and statistically relevant when interpreting an aggregate transcriptomic score.

Controls also examined whether shrinkage worked through its alignment with the learned correction. None of 200 permutations of readout weights within expression/variance strata matched the observed rectangle improvement. Applying the weights to matched-norm random increments likewise failed to improve on the baseline in those draws. These conditional diagnostics support the specific pairing of response structure and measurement-derived amplitudes. They complement the uniform shrinkage comparison while leaving the held-out prediction, including its concentrated readout benefit, as the primary outcome.

Independent-source checks further separated baseline accuracy from partner transfer. In a Dixit RNA evaluation, the complete baseline improved over additivity by 23.43%, while the extra correction gave a 0.270% complete-response improvement and a −0.033% partner gain.[17] A frozen Combi-seq drug-program evaluation also lacked an additional partner improvement.[18] Together with the positive matched-panel result, these findings identify a concrete capability and its present range: explicit partner structure can improve prediction, but its transfer depends on the readout, measurement support and experimental context.

### Combination support enables prediction of functional growth responses

Growth phenotypes provide a complementary application with a direct functional readout and denser combination coverage. The analysis used repeat-specific measurements from the Horlbeck genetic-interaction study.[4] Each repeat had a baseline fitted from permitted single and double measurements. A symmetric matrix then modelled the mean residual shared across repeats. A signed rank-one matrix was the primary correction; a spectral-shrinkage matrix provided a more flexible secondary implementation.[19] Selection used training combinations, and both corrections were evaluated against the baseline refitted for the same observations.

For eighty K562 genes, low and high training budgets contained 421–422 and 2,317–2,318 pairs per fold. The 632 evaluated pairs stayed fixed across the three folds. Increasing support raised the rank-one correction's complete-response gain from 0.27% to 11.03%, while its partner gain rose from −0.033% to 11.68%. The high-budget spectral correction gave complete and partner gains of 11.39% and 10.67%. The baseline itself improved by 10.12% when supplied with additional observations. The data therefore supported separate benefits for the baseline and for the added partner structure.

These budgets change condition count, endpoint degree and layout coverage together. Their value lies in demonstrating that more extensive combination measurements can support a transferable residual prediction on fixed queries. The controlled mechanism experiment isolates layout information at equal budget; the growth experiment tests the return from expanding real experimental support. Considering both avoids attributing every biological data-volume effect to a single structural property of the design.

The procedure was frozen before evaluation in Jurkat, using 79 genes, 2,464 training pairs and 617 held-out pairs. Model coefficients were estimated from Jurkat training data. This tests portability of the procedure to another cellular environment within the same study. The primary rank-one correction reduced pooled growth-response error by 6.43% and endpoint-eliminated partner error by 6.18%. Complete gains were 4.45% and 8.88% in the two measured repeats. The spectral correction achieved gains of 8.16% and 5.90%, respectively. Removing each endpoint from scoring left complete rank-one gains between 4.95% and 7.10%.

Illustrative pairs show the practical meaning of the correction. For TTI2–PGK1, the mean baseline prediction was −0.274 against an observed response of −0.321; the correction moved it to −0.287. For TTI2–OGFOD1, the baseline was −0.069 against −0.036, and the correction moved it to −0.058. The shared TTI2 endpoint receives corrections in opposite directions depending on its partner. These descriptively selected examples explain a prediction already evaluated across all pairs; they do not establish the molecular mechanism behind the response.

For a user of a partial growth screen, the workflow is complete: provide measured singles and combinations, fit the baseline and residual matrix, and obtain functional predictions for remaining pairs. The held-out measurements assess the utility of that output. This application gives the common response construction a functional interpretation independent of any RNA-based recommendation. It shows that a modest structured correction, when supported by sufficient measured combinations, can improve an experimentally meaningful phenotype.

The signed low-rank structure permits buffering and aggravating corrections through a shared statistical representation. Its interpretation is a pattern of functional residuals across partners, with the global evaluation establishing whether that pattern generalizes. The two repeats test whether gains persist across repeated phenotype measurements, and the second cell environment tests the portability of the fitting procedure. These different comparisons connect a compact computational construction to a useful functional outcome without requiring the matrix factors to be assigned a molecular identity.

### Risk selection and PairPick connect predictions to research decisions

Two distinct uses connect response prediction to subsequent research operations. Risk ranking asks which predictions are more dependable within a specified evaluation setting. Partner selection asks which candidate best matches an explicit objective. Their validation uses different outputs and denominators, so each application is evaluated through its own completed procedure.

An analytic-risk implementation was calibrated on development pairs and evaluated on 213 records from 33 pair identities absent from calibration. A fixed threshold retained 110 records at 49.48% pair-balanced coverage. Accepted predictions had an MSE ratio of 0.5343 relative to all predictions, with a pair-cluster bootstrap interval of 0.3646–0.7591. A separate empirical ranking of the released predictor showed an 8.27% improvement over an endpoint-exposure control at 50% coverage in Wessels; the corresponding Norman difference was unresolved. These analyses support selective use in the tested settings and distinguish empirical risk ranking from a general error guarantee.

WitnessCell–PairPick provides a different operation: a user specifies an anchor intervention and a transcriptional objective, and predicted candidate responses are scored against that objective. The default evaluated R-G configuration supplies the response predictions. In the CRISPRai source GSE220974, the comparison included 28 fixed-anchor tasks over 49 detected mode-specific double labels, with activation and repression distinguished.[20] The five primary methods shared candidate pools and physical-pair holdouts. GEARS and Scouter each completed five folds and three seeds, with their prediction means scored as the ensemble.

PairPick (R-G) had mean regret 0.006197 and selected the observed best candidate in 19 of 28 tasks. Standard linear prediction, Scouter, GEARS and single-response addition had regrets of 0.006591, 0.006623, 0.010258 and 0.012220, with 18, 17, 18 and 18 hits. Standard linear prediction had the lowest RNA MSE, and GEARS the lowest partner MSE. An archived PRESAGE comparator made the same 28 choices as R-G. Thus the application demonstrates useful recommendations and the distinction between prediction and choice metrics, with limited evidence for superiority across all comparators.

These tasks arise from eight physical anchors, and their detected labels have not all been verified against the intended-construct whitelist. The application is a retrospective source comparison with descriptive uncertainty. Its complete use case remains concrete: supply existing perturbation measurements, define a response objective, obtain a partner recommendation and assess the achieved objective on a held-out candidate pool. The endpoint is transcriptional utility in that pool; the functional growth application above has its own measured outcome.

A response improvement can leave a recommendation unchanged when all methods preserve the same candidate ordering. Conversely, a small error along the chosen objective can change which partner is selected despite similar transcriptome-wide error. Reporting achieved utility and regret alongside RNA and partner losses makes this distinction visible. PairPick consequently offers an operational use of the predicted response whose benefit is measured at the decision endpoint, rather than inferred from an unrelated accuracy percentage.

## Discussion

WitnessCell connects the information in combination experiments to a practical response-prediction method. Its released core performs strongly in a defined multi-dataset benchmark, and its explicit partner extensions test an additional capability beyond endpoint refinement. The causal analysis gives these results a common scientific context: a prediction depends on whether the available interventions distinguish the relevant answers and whether the model can estimate and transfer the resulting information. These questions are related, but each requires its own evidence.

The causal contribution is target-specific. A joint intervention can distinguish explanations that survive every permitted single intervention and thereby resolve an unmeasured response within a stated mechanism class. This gives experimental support a meaning beyond sample count or endpoint coverage. The equal-budget comparison establishes that two apparently comparable screens can have different intrinsic prediction limits. It also explains why successful discrimination need not imply recovery of a complete regulatory network. For a researcher, the important object can be a particular combination response or partner contrast.

The computational contribution makes the learned capability inspectable. Endpoint refinement, nonlinear composition and explicit partner correction can all improve a delivered response, and the projection separates their effects. The matched RNA result shows that repeated training measurements can help control an added partner component. Its concentration in a small readout subset and the independent-source results specify where that gain currently applies. The growth experiments show a complementary setting in which denser support enables partner prediction and a frozen procedure retains value in another cellular environment.

This combination of analyses suggests a practical way to develop and compare future predictors. A complete-response improvement should first be localized to the components it changes. An added partner component should then be assessed on contrasts that the preceding endpoint correction cannot alter. Repeated measurements can reveal whether its useful directions are stable enough to estimate, and an explicit research objective can determine whether the resulting prediction changes a decision. The approach preserves strong aggregate performance as an important outcome while making the additional scientific capability falsifiable. It can also identify situations in which improving a baseline is the most useful computational contribution for the data available.

The biological significance follows from the responses that become predictable. In transcriptomic screens, the method can improve downstream expression estimates while distinguishing concentrated partner-sensitive changes from broader response accuracy. In functional screens, the same baseline-plus-residual principle supports prediction of growth effects that depend on the partner. In a selection task, the objective determines which aspects of a response matter for the recommended experiment. These are complementary routes from existing measurements to research value. Their connection rests on a common information and response construction, with each biological outcome evaluated in its own experimental setting.

Several boundaries guide use. The causal result depends on the candidate mechanism family and stipulated interventions. RNA sampling halves share experimental factors. Jurkat tests a refitted procedure within the Horlbeck study, and the selection comparison has few physical anchors and an unresolved construct whitelist. The response, risk and ranking implementations also have distinct fitted parameters. These boundaries favour reporting an explicit prediction target, method configuration and experimental context whenever a result is used to guide further work.

A future extension is to choose the next measurement that resolves an important target ambiguity. Active-learning methods provide a foundation for this goal.[21,22] The present information result motivates such selection, while existing one-measurement acquisition tests retained in the archive did not confirm an outer-target improvement. The practical contribution established here is the prediction and analysis of responses from available support. By connecting causal distinguishability, measurable response components and held-out utility, WitnessCell provides a basis for using partial combination screens more effectively and for locating the information needed to extend their predictive reach.

## Methods

### Study design and implementation identities

The study reanalysed public perturbation measurements and used a specified synthetic causal system. No new biological samples were collected. The primary response method is the released WitnessCell transcriptomic core. The matched RNA partner predictor, functional growth model, analytic-risk predictor and empirical risk ranking are separately fitted implementations. WitnessCell–PairPick names the partner-selection application; its default response configuration is the archived R-G algorithm. PairPick-Stable and PairPick-TargetRank retain their separate identities. Naming changes do not alter fitted predictions, losses or archived model identifiers.

Biological prediction splits assign unordered perturbation pairs, keeping associated cells, guides, orientations and repeated measurements together. Permitted singles remain available. Mixed activation/repression tasks additionally group mode variants by physical gene pair. Inner fitting reconstructs preprocessing, baseline and correction from the allowed observations. Development panels, procedure-transfer evaluations and source-transfer comparisons retain their original status. Repeated optimization seeds, overlapping folds, shared-source datasets and algebraic contrast dimensions are not counted as independent biological replications. The archive preserves original failures, subsequent development and local prediction freezes.

### Data sources and response definitions

The released benchmark used scPerturBench-processed Norman, Wessels, Schmidt and Replogle_exp6 datasets, their Gene Ontology support filters and public split seeds 1–3.[10] Primary experimental sources include Norman GSE133344, Wessels GSE213957 and Replogle GSE146194; Schmidt retains the benchmark's processed labels with the primary T-cell study as provenance.[3,14,15,16] Benchmark and matched CaRPool analyses share the Wessels source and constitute different tasks, not independent source replication. The full benchmark contains 540 combination–seed and 114 unseen-single records.

RNA effects are condition means relative to the permitted control reference. Source preprocessing and gene axes are retained for each experiment. The matched CaRPool task has 55 pairs, fourteen singles, five outer folds, eleven same-fit evaluation rectangles and 4,995 non-target readouts. Its neural comparators retain their 5,020-gene model axis and use the same scoring mask. Growth phenotypes are repeat-specific Triple Sequencing measurements from GSE116198.[4] Independent checks use Dixit GSE90063/GSM2396860 and Combi-seq GSE174695.[17,18] The mixed-mode selection application uses GSE220974.[20]

### Single-response estimation and the released transcriptomic core

For a measured single $g$, the effect is $e_g=\mu_g-\mu_0$. Missing endpoints use a head combining the equal-condition mean of training singles, a ten-neighbour Jaccard-weighted Gene Ontology response and a self-response anchor. Leave-one-single feature construction excludes the endpoint being fitted. Coefficients use numerical ridge $10^{-8}$ and clipping to $[0,2]$. The additional on-target amplitude head uses generic self response and sign-matched tail and root-mean-square fingerprint features, relative ridge 0.2 and coefficient bounds $[-1.5,1.5]$.

For every outer training endpoint, nested checks remove that endpoint before fitting the base head and amplitude correction. Activation requires positive one-sided 95% lower bounds for both relative all-gene MSE gain and top-100 correlation gain; top-100 MSE is recorded but is not a third gate. Fewer than six usable endpoints retains the preceding head. Measured endpoints are unchanged. This amplitude extension is excluded from selection of the released combination hyperparameters.

With $e^{\mathrm{base}}$ and $e^{\mathrm{final}}$ denoting the preceding and final endpoint effects, the released predictor is

### RNA partner kernel and training-measurement shrinkage

For single effects on $p$ readouts, define $L_{gh}=e_g^\top e_h/p$ and the symmetric product kernel

$$K_{(g,h),(u,v)}=\tfrac12(L_{gu}L_{hv}+L_{gv}L_{hu}).$$

With $P_T=I-X_TX_T^+$ and $A_Q=X_QX_T^+$, the training and query blocks are $P_TK_{TT}P_T$ and $(K_{QT}-A_QK_{TT})P_T$. Both are divided by the projected training trace per pair. A degenerate trace returns zero correction. Ridge regression fits residuals from a baseline $M_0$ containing saturation and endpoint refinement. Three inner folds select ridge from $\{.1,1,10\}$ by available complete-rectangle loss, using complete-response loss when no rectangle exists; ties favour stronger regularization. Every inner fit rebuilds $M_0$ from its permitted observations.

Disjoint training cell halves supply projected responses $Z_1=P_TY_T^{(1)}$ and $Z_2=P_TY_T^{(2)}$. The readout multiplier is

$$w_j=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\frac{Z_{1,\cdot j}^{\top}Z_{2,\cdot j}}{(\|Z_{1,\cdot j}\|^2+\|Z_{2,\cdot j}\|^2)/2},\qquad \widehat Y_R=M_0+G\operatorname{diag}(w).$$

Zero denominators yield zero weights. The equivalent half-mean/half-difference energy expression is an algebraic identity, not an independent control. Shared experimental effects can persist in both halves. The multiplier is therefore a shrinkage coefficient, not a calibrated probability. It scales predictions before evaluation on a common unweighted axis.

A uniform control fits $c\in[0,1]$ for $M_0+cG$ from fully refitted inner correction/error products. ### Risk ranking and selective prediction

The analytic implementation uses $y=X\beta+u+\varepsilon$, with $\Sigma=\operatorname{Cov}(u+\varepsilon)$, $k_t=\operatorname{Cov}(u+\varepsilon,u_t)$ and $k_{tt}=\operatorname{Var}(u_t)$. Under the unbiasedness constraint $X^\top a=t$, the minimum-risk linear predictor has coefficient

$$a_t=\Sigma^{-1}k_t+\Sigma^{-1}X(X^\top\Sigma^{-1}X)^{-1}(t-X^\top\Sigma^{-1}k_t),$$

and model-based risk

$$W_t=k_{tt}-k_t^\top\Sigma^{-1}k_t+\widetilde t^\top(X^\top\Sigma^{-1}X)^{-1}\widetilde t,\qquad\widetilde t=t-X^\top\Sigma^{-1}k_t.$$

These expressions use a full-column-rank endpoint design and the stated second-moment model.[12,13] A descriptor kernel fitted from permitted single-response programs supplies train–query covariance through standardized principal components, symmetric pair descriptors and an RBF discrepancy component. The implementation is distinct from the released endpoint predictor and the RNA partner extension. Its empirical calibration does not establish distribution-free coverage.

Coverage is accepted weight divided by total weight; accepted error is accepted weighted loss divided by accepted weight. Uncertainty resamples pair identities. The weighted complete-case follow-up uses 207 records from 32 pairs without changing the threshold or accepted identities.
