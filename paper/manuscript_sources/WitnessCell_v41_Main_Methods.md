# WitnessCell predicts combinatorial perturbation responses through endpoint and partner learning

## Abstract

Joint perturbation measurements can improve combination prediction through endpoint refinement and changes in partner contrasts. WitnessCell learns these contributions and uses internal held-out combinations to regulate partner corrections while preserving endpoint contributions. A controlled causal analysis shows how joint-intervention layouts resolve target ambiguity, and the released predictor ranks first in aggregate evaluation across four datasets. Functional analyses distinguish shared single-effect nonlinearities from additional predictive structure. In K562, rank-one correction reduces partner error by 11.32% beyond a fitted saturation reference. In an independent study, Han spectral correction reduces partner error by 4.82% beyond the same reference family. In Jurkat, an explicit global term accounts for much of the original gain; RNA shrinkage preserves concentrated improvements and reduces adverse corrections. WitnessCell provides competitive response prediction and a practical way to test and control what added response models contribute.

Combinatorial perturbation screens measure how a cell's response to one intervention changes when another is added. Predicting unmeasured combinations would extend these screens and help researchers study gene function across cellular contexts.[1,2,3] A practical starting point is a collection of single-perturbation measurements and a partially measured combination screen. The goal is to predict the remaining expression or functional responses. A reduction in overall prediction error, however, leaves an important question open: did the model estimate each intervention's contribution more accurately, or did it learn changes that depend on the partner?

Existing methods combine intervention representations, transform cellular states or use gene relationships to predict responses.[4,5,6,7,8] Large-scale benchmarks and strong simple references test whether these predictions improve on alternatives.[9,10] Systema examines whether predictions capture perturbation-specific changes beyond systematic variation, while Miller and colleagues test whether evaluation metrics distinguish informative predictions.[11,12] For combinations, a further question concerns the source of an improvement. Measurements may reveal that a model consistently underestimates one intervention's contribution across several partners. Correcting that shared error improves prediction, but leaves open whether the model also predicts how exchanging partners changes the response in different backgrounds.

Consider replacing partner B with D, first alongside intervention A and then alongside C. If the replacement has different effects in the two backgrounds, predicting that difference requires more than refining the separate contributions of A, B, C and D. We call this difference a partner contrast. This contrast tests whether joint measurements improve prediction of partner-dependent changes beyond contributions shared across combinations. Such changes can arise from a shared nonlinear response to single-effect strength as well as from gene-associated structure; distinguishing these sources requires a further reference comparison.

WitnessCell addresses this distinction within a prediction method. Its core combines single responses nonlinearly and learns a correction from measured combinations. We show that this correction refines contributions associated with individual intervention identities, termed endpoints, while leaving partner contrasts unchanged. We then construct additional corrections that can change those contrasts and test them against the same fitted baseline. A controlled causal system examines the information needed for this task: the choice of joint measurements can determine whether mechanisms giving different answers to an unmeasured combination can be distinguished.[13]

WitnessCell further uses this distinction to control learned predictions: it retains the endpoint contribution of an added correction and determines partner strength from internal held-out combinations. The benchmark evaluates complete prediction, while functional and RNA experiments test the additional learners against their own fitted baselines. Functional comparisons examine whether gains survive explicit global references and extend to a separate study. RNA experiments test how training-measurement consistency guides correction across transcripts. Together, these analyses link a competitive predictor to two additional capabilities: testing what an added response model contributes and controlling how that contribution enters a prediction. The supplied interfaces support fitting, missing-combination prediction and component-specific adjustment.

## Results

### WitnessCell separates response components and controls partner corrections

WitnessCell starts from control and available single-perturbation measurements, observed combinations and the identities of requested combinations. Its transcriptomic core, the configuration used in the benchmark, uses measured single effects when available and estimates missing ones from training responses and gene annotations. It combines the single effects through a saturation rule, then transfers residuals from measured combinations that share endpoints with the query (Fig. 1a). Training or validation measurements determine the fitted settings. All measurements from each test combination are excluded from model fitting. The resulting predictor can use combination data even when some requested single effects have not been measured.

Nonlinear composition allows the joint prediction to depart from the sum of the single responses. Residual transfer then estimates the errors associated with particular endpoints across measured combinations. For example, several combinations containing A may consistently exceed the nonlinear prediction; the residual branch can transfer an A-associated correction to another pair containing A. Such a correction can improve the complete response: the prediction across all readouts before endpoint removal. Whether it also improves partner contrasts depends on how that correction changes when partners are exchanged.

To identify what the transferred correction learns, consider four combinations: A with B or D, and C with B or D. The contrast

$$\Delta=Y_{AB}-Y_{AD}-Y_{CB}+Y_{CD}$$

asks whether exchanging B for D has the same effect in backgrounds A and C. An endpoint contribution describes the intervention's contribution across all measured readouts, including downstream genes; it is broader than the targeted gene's own RNA response.

The cancellation can be seen by writing an endpoint-only response as $Y_{AB}=a_A+a_B$. The contribution $a_A$ enters both AB and AD and disappears in their difference; $a_C$ disappears in the corresponding CB and CD difference. Both differences then equal $a_B-a_D$, which also cancels when the backgrounds are compared. A nonzero contrast records a background-dependent change in the partner exchange. This comparison removes a separate contribution for each endpoint, whereas subtracting one common response across all perturbations removes only their shared change. The four measurements therefore test the partner contrast directly.

This cancellation extends to a collection of query pairs. Let $X_Q$ indicate the two endpoints present in each pair. Classical projection gives $P_Q=I-X_QX_Q^+$, which removes sums of endpoint contributions.[14,15] The released residual branch has the form $\delta_Q=X_Q\Theta$, so

$$P_Q\delta_Q=0.$$

This identity specifies the response component changed by the endpoint-based residual-transfer branch: complete predictions can improve while their endpoint-eliminated part is preserved. The nonlinear baseline can already produce partner contrasts. An explicit partner learner is therefore tested against that actual baseline to quantify its added predictive value beyond nonlinear composition (Fig. 1b,d).

The projector removes the best-fitting sum of endpoint contributions across all query pairs. Four-corner rectangles provide the simplest example, but longer cycles can also support such contrasts. A query-pair graph that is a tree can be explained entirely by freely fitted endpoint contributions; closing suitable cycles creates contrasts that those contributions cannot absorb. The arrangement of queried pairs therefore determines which partner responses can be tested. Global nonlinear responses that survive this separation are examined with the matched references below.

WitnessCell adds corrections through separately fitted RNA and functional learners (Fig. 1c). The RNA learner uses similarity between the two single-response profiles of a query and those of measured pairs, allowing either endpoint ordering. It removes endpoint contributions from the training problem before estimating the correction. The primary functional learner fits a signed symmetric matrix to residual growth responses, sharing patterns across partners. These constructions can change partner contrasts. A learner of pair-dependent responses can also produce a component shared across an endpoint’s partners. Projecting its output separates this endpoint refinement from the additional partner variation, allowing both contributions to be evaluated.

To improve a partner contrast, the correction must align with the baseline's remaining error and have an appropriate magnitude. A single strength selected by complete-response error can favor a useful endpoint change despite an adverse partner change. Controlling the two parts separately addresses this mismatch.

For a baseline $M_0$ and learned correction $D$, WitnessCell can retain the endpoint part and adjust only the partner part:

$$\widehat Y_\alpha=M_0+(I-P_Q)D+\alpha P_QD.$$

Predictions for internally held-out combinations determine $\alpha$ between zero and one (Fig. 1e). The operation preserves the endpoint contribution exactly. Under the same unweighted squared-error measure, a decrease in partner sum of squared errors (SSE) relative to the original predictor produces an equal decrease in complete-response SSE. Internal measurements choose the strength; outer held-out outcomes test whether that choice improves prediction. Supplementary Note 17 gives the derivation and explains why the optimal complete-response strength can differ from the optimal partner strength.

Users choose a configuration from their measurements and prediction objective before examining query outcomes. Normalized RNA summaries support the core; training cell halves additionally support the RNA partner extension; repeated functional measurements support the growth model. Each implementation provides a baseline and an added correction, with its own parameters, training objective and permitted fitting data. The control interface adjusts a specified query panel without its measured outcomes. Supplementary Table S29 specifies inputs and outputs, and Table S30 records the supporting experiments' evaluation status.

### Joint interventions resolve target ambiguities left by single perturbations

We first asked whether the training measurements determine the partner response of interest. We examined this requirement in a controlled causal system with a source, perturbed mediators and a downstream response. A direct source effect and an effect passing through a hidden mediator gate can produce identical responses under control and all permitted single knockouts. Knocking out an appropriate pair breaks that agreement: the gate turns off while the source remains active (Fig. 2a). The joint intervention reveals a difference that additional observations of the same single knockouts cannot resolve.

Mechanisms can fit all observed conditions equally well yet predict different unmeasured responses. The data identify a query when compatible mechanisms agree on its answer, even if their internal parameters differ. We therefore examine identification of the requested response, matching the theoretical question to the prediction task. This distinction separates uncertainty in the experimental information from the estimator's ability to use it.

We compared two sets of joint measurements while keeping the number of conditions, observations and appearances of every endpoint identical. Both excluded the same four query pairs (Fig. 2b). In the comparison set, four possible mechanisms generated identical training distributions but disagreed on the requested partner contrast. This imposes an estimator-independent squared-error floor of 0.0625. Repeating those conditions more precisely preserves the ambiguity. The informative set instead distinguished the mechanisms responsible for the different query answers (Fig. 2c).

Changing only the measurement set reduced target error by 92.84% for the same least-squares procedure (Fig. 2d). A strong generalized least-squares estimator also benefited from the informative measurements. The gain therefore reflects what the experiments reveal about the query, rather than an advantage exclusive to one estimator. Identification depends on the specified mechanism family; allowing additional simultaneous gates restores ambiguity, as shown by a counterexample in Supplementary Note 2.

The experiment shows why endpoint coverage and observation count alone do not determine the information available for an unmeasured combination. Which interventions are measured together can matter even at the same budget. The biological analyses next ask how WitnessCell uses available combination measurements, first to improve complete predictions and then to learn additional partner-dependent responses.

### WitnessCell leads aggregate evaluation of unmeasured combinations

We evaluated the WitnessCell core in the genetic-combination track of scPerturBench, using its four datasets and original six-metric aggregation.[9] The comparison included fifteen eligible comparator entries, including simple references, alongside WitnessCell. The complete track combines double perturbations and unseen singles; we also evaluated the 540 double-perturbation records separately, retaining the same methods, scores and aggregation rule. These datasets include combinatorial expression, CaRPool, primary T-cell and direct-capture perturbation measurements.[1,16,2,17]

WitnessCell ranked first in both the complete track and the double-only subset. For doubles, its mean operation rank was 3.515, compared with 5.150 for the next-ranked method, Scouter.[18] The paired difference had a 95% cluster-bootstrap interval of −2.117 to −1.141 (Fig. 3a,c). All fifteen comparator differences had pointwise intervals below zero. WitnessCell also ranked first by point estimate when neither, one or both query endpoints had been seen in training (Fig. 3b). Complete rankings and paired intervals for these subsets of the benchmark are reported in Supplementary Tables S25–S26.

The aggregate comparison summarizes ranks across metrics, while MSE measures error magnitude in each dataset's original response units. These views reveal different strengths. WitnessCell led the combined evaluation in three datasets and ranked fourth in Schmidt. Its MSE was lower than Scouter's in Norman and Replogle_exp6 but higher in Wessels and Schmidt (Fig. 3d). Wasserstein and common differential-expression rankings were also weaker, and the linear model led on unseen singles. Together, aggregate ranks and original-scale errors describe complementary aspects of prediction.

Additional analyses examined how the core uses combination measurements. Improvements persisted after removing directly manipulated RNA coordinates, showing that downstream readouts benefited. Holding the nonlinear baseline fixed and increasing the available Wessels combination measurements improved complete-response accuracy (Supplementary Fig. S3). This comparison isolates the contribution of the residual library from changes to the single-response composition. Supplementary Note 15 gives the effect sizes and separately defined training-mean reference analysis; Methods and Table S31 define the official reference entries and report sensitivity to their inclusion.

The same saved predictions also confirmed the endpoint identity above: residual transfer left the projected partner error unchanged within numerical precision (Fig. 1d; Supplementary Table S9). Thus, a useful improvement in complete prediction can occur without an additional partner correction. This observation motivates a direct test of whether an explicit partner learner improves unmeasured functional responses.

### Functional gains distinguish global nonlinearities, partner structure and control

Growth measurements test the added learner against a directly measured cellular phenotype. Using the Horlbeck genetic-interaction screen,[3] WitnessCell fits repeat-specific baselines to single and combination measurements, then predicts residual growth through a shared signed matrix. Rank one is the primary correction; a spectral model provides a secondary comparison.[19] Each is tested against its corresponding refitted baseline. Building on earlier fitness prediction work, including GEARS,[8] we test whether partner learning improves a functional predictor that already uses combination measurements.

During K562 development, we compared nested training pools while keeping each fold's query pairs fixed. The low pool contained 421–422 pairs and the high pool 2,317–2,318. Baseline and correction were refitted at each budget. The primary partner gain changed from approximately zero to 11.68%; complete-response gain at high support was 11.03% (Fig. 4a). Comparing with a same-budget baseline isolates the additional correction from improvements obtained simply by giving the baseline more data. The comparison shows a benefit of expanding experimental support for this learner. Condition count, endpoint coverage and layout changed together; Supplementary Notes 7 and 11 describe the designs and analysis stages.

We next fixed the model family and selection rules before fitting Jurkat. The primary correction reduced complete-response error by 6.43% and partner error by 6.18% across 617 held-out pairs (Fig. 4b), with positive gains in both measured repeats. Coefficients were estimated anew from 2,464 measured pairs, approximately 80% of possible pairs among 79 endpoints. This tests transfer of the fitting procedure between cellular environments within one study and supports completion of an already substantially measured screen. A four-combination Jurkat example illustrates the predicted partner contrast (Supplementary Fig. S7e,f).

A partner contrast establishes a departure from additive endpoint contributions, but does not identify its biological source. Global epistasis can arise when a shared nonlinear response transforms otherwise additive inputs.[44] For example, curvature in a response to $s_g+s_h$ produces a term proportional to $s_gs_h$. We examined two global references: an explicit single-effect product and a saturating transformation of the single-effect sum, each fitted using permitted training combinations. For the first, we added one unpenalized product coefficient per repeat to the original baseline, jointly refitted its other coefficients, and reapplied the same internal selection rules and zero-correction option. This matched comparison tests the learner after a simple global cross-term is available.

The matched reference revealed different sources of improvement in the two cellular settings. K562 high-support rank-one partner gain remained 11.51% beyond the expanded baseline, close to the original 11.68%. In Jurkat, the expanded global baseline alone reduced partner error by 7.13% relative to the original baseline, exceeding the original rank-one predictor's 6.18% improvement. Refitting rank one and the spectral learner added 0.41% and 1.31%, respectively, relative to the expanded baseline (Fig. 4c). Thus, a directly fitted global term takes over much of Jurkat's original correction while improving prediction. Table S54 gives both learner-specific gains and a common-reference comparison of the final predictors.

We next applied the saturation reference to the three K562 high-support folds and Jurkat, fitting it within each permitted training subset. All partner-model settings and original query sets were retained. K562 rank-one correction reduced partner error by 11.32% beyond this reference, with gains in each fold and measured repeat. Jurkat selected zero rank-one correction, while the spectral learner added 1.99%. The K562 increment therefore persisted under both the product and saturation references. Supplementary Table S54 reports the corresponding complete-response errors and all controlled outputs.

The fitted rank-one loadings linked the K562 correction to individual genes. Their absolute Pearson correlations with mean single effects were below 0.03 across the three high-support folds, compared with 0.795 in Jurkat. Genes associated with mitochondrial translation and respiration, including MRPL33, MRPL3, MRPL43 and NDUFA8, had high loadings (Fig. 4d). Pairs involving a descriptive sixteen-gene mitochondrial set comprised 33–38% of queries and accounted for 66–80% of the original projected error reduction; the concentration persisted after product-reference refitting. These gene-associated predictive patterns connect the learned correction to specific functional annotations.[45] Table S56 reports the complete gene set and contributions.

We next tested the functional learning procedure in Han's independent K562 growth screen.[43] The established model families, regularization choices, internal selection rule and zero-correction option were fixed before fitting the Han models. Model parameters were then estimated from the allowed Han combinations. The reconstructed panel contained 20,359 pairs and two end-sample repeats; five three-fold partitions supplied separate training and query pairs. A related published-phenotype panel assessed sensitivity to response construction. The rank-one model remained primary and the spectral model secondary throughout this evaluation.

Against its own fitted functional baseline, the primary rank-one model reduced reconstructed complete-response error by 9.09% and partner error by 1.91%. The published phenotype also showed positive gains. The spectral correction reduced partner error by 5.65% and 7.88% in the reconstructed and published panels, respectively (Fig. 4e). Both learners improved partner prediction in all five partitions, and the reconstructed repeats agreed in direction. These results extend the useful functional increment from the Horlbeck study to a separate experimental source through local parameter estimation. Supplementary Note 21 reports all folds, both readouts and the fixed analysis hierarchy.

A stronger comparison tested whether Han's increment remained after fitting a nonlinear response to single-effect strength. On the same permitted training pairs, the saturation reference fitted endpoint contributions and a transformed single-response sum. Its settings were selected inside each training subset before partner learning. Spectral corrections reduced partner error by 4.82% in reconstructed responses and 7.04% in published phenotypes (Fig. 4f). The rank-one correction retained a smaller reconstructed benefit, while the published analysis selected zero. The prespecified secondary spectral family therefore supplied the most consistent increment beyond this reference. Tables S45–S50 report all selections, absolute reference losses and complete-response gains.

We then decomposed the entire saved Han correction into the single-product direction and its orthogonal remainder. Almost all of the spectral improvement beyond saturation lay in the remainder: 4.79 percentage points in reconstructed responses and 7.03 in published phenotypes, on their respective saturation-baseline denominators. This particular global product direction therefore accounted for little of the retained spectral benefit. Table S57 gives the exact decomposition for both learner families and both references.

Learning a useful correction also raises a separate question: how much of each response component should enter the final prediction? In the independent Shen study, the original functional procedure improved complete-response prediction in HeLa, A549 and 293T while slightly increasing partner error.[20] Its endpoint component supplied the overall benefit. Applying one attenuation coefficient to the entire correction would therefore link a useful endpoint change to a less useful partner change.

We compared whole attenuation and component control using the same internally estimated partner coefficient. Whole attenuation reduced partner damage but removed much of Shen's complete-response benefit. Component control retained the endpoint contribution and achieved higher complete-response gains in each background (Fig. 4g). The same rule retained Jurkat's original gain. Supplementary Notes 18–19 report the complete comparison across all eligible configurations. This evaluation demonstrates the value of controlling the two contributions separately when their validation evidence differs.

Han provides a further test on locally learned, useful partner directions. Using the internally estimated strength increased the native rank-one model's partner gain to 2.44% for reconstructed responses and 2.77% for published phenotypes, with positive gains in every partition. Complete-response accuracy improved at the same time (Fig. 4h). Component control also outperformed an independently selected whole-correction coefficient optimized for internal complete-response error in this configuration. The correction retained a nonzero partner contribution, so its benefit went beyond removing the partner term. Most spectral coefficients were already close to one; attenuation supplied little extra benefit there and slightly reduced gains over the saturation reference. Supplementary Tables S48–S49 distinguish these cases.

Training-side repeatability and fixed-direction diagnostics help interpret these differences. Horlbeck and Han showed greater endpoint-eliminated repeat agreement than Shen, yet the low-support K562 fits did not gain from the added learner. The value of repeatable measurements also depends on the fitting procedure and pair layout. Even the best scalar chosen using held-out Shen responses offered little additional partner gain along the existing directions. This retrospective bound concerns adjustment of a fixed direction, while learning a different direction remains a separate task. Supplementary Note 23 separates these retrospective quantities from training-derived coefficients.

The functional results connect learning and use. K562 establishes a substantial increment that persists across the tested global references; Jurkat shows how a simpler global term can take over much of a learned correction; Han extends functional partner prediction to another study. Shen and native Han show how response separation guides the use of an existing correction, motivating the transcript-specific adjustment examined next. Methods and Table S30 specify the evaluation status of these reanalyses.

### Readout shrinkage reduces adverse RNA corrections

The RNA extension asks whether training measurements can regulate partner correction across thousands of transcripts. We evaluated it on the matched CaRPool dataset used for model development.[16] Whole combinations were held out, and eleven four-pair rectangles compared predictions from the same fitted model. The baseline already included nonlinear composition and endpoint refinement. The additional kernel estimates corrections from endpoint-eliminated training responses using similarities between pairs' single-response profiles. Its held-out rectangle error tests the added prediction of partner contrasts.

The usefulness of a correction can differ between transcripts. A common amplitude may preserve a strong response change but also carry weak or inconsistent changes into other readouts. WitnessCell compares endpoint-eliminated responses in two disjoint halves of the training cells and assigns smaller corrections to transcripts with weaker agreement. This agreement measures sampling consistency within the source experiment. A training-selected uniform shrinkage control tests whether transcript-specific weighting adds value beyond reducing all corrections by one factor. Held-out combinations then provide a separate test of the training-derived adjustment; Methods specifies the measurement scope.

The resulting predictor, R, reduced held-out rectangle error by 15.96% relative to its baseline and by 6.94% relative to uniform shrinkage (Fig. 5b,c). Transcript-specific weighting therefore added value beyond a common shrinkage factor. An error-budget analysis separated transcripts with reduced error from those with increased error, determining the sign separately for each model. R preserved most of the unweighted correction's total positive error reduction while markedly lowering its total adverse increment (Fig. 5d). Supplementary Notes 6, 13 and 15 provide the full budgets, matched neural comparisons and individual-seed results.

The largest benefits concerned a defined group of strongly changing readouts. Ten transcripts, including S100A9, S100A8, S100A12 and LYZ, accounted for over 90% of positive error reductions. Removing that fixed set or standardizing by training-condition variance eliminated the aggregate gain (Fig. 5e,f). These checks distinguish magnitude from breadth: standardization reduces the influence of large-scale discrepancies, while deletion tests prediction outside the selected transcripts. Outside that set, shrinkage substantially reduced the harm caused by the unweighted correction, leaving the net gain close to zero and slightly negative. Thus, its useful RNA improvement was concentrated, while its reduction of adverse corrections extended to other readouts.

Training-based shrinkage improved the balance of useful and adverse corrections in the CaRPool development dataset. Fixed-procedure evaluations in Dixit and Combi-seq did not establish the same added partner benefit, although the Dixit baseline provided useful complete-response prediction.[21,22] Table S36 places these outcomes alongside the corresponding training counts, readouts and evaluation status. Supplementary Note 20 extends the control comparison to all 265 eligible saved RNA candidate records on their separate full-query projection task. The eleven-rectangle result above remains the primary RNA comparison.

## Discussion

Combination measurements improve prediction by refining contributions shared across an intervention's partners and by revealing changes in partner contrasts. WitnessCell turns this distinction into a complete prediction method with explicit additional learners and a component-control operation. Its released core supplies competitive transcriptomic prediction. Functional and RNA experiments then establish what the added learners contribute beyond their fitted baselines. The resulting method connects prediction accuracy to the response component being learned and to a practical decision about how strongly that component should enter the output.

The causal construction shows that equal budgets and endpoint coverage can leave different uncertainty about an unmeasured target. The endpoint identity answers a complementary question: can a particular output branch express the response difference of interest? These results distinguish information available in the experiment from capacity supplied by the model. Held-out biological measurements then test how much of either is realized in prediction.

Global-reference comparisons sharpen the biological interpretation and the division of work within the predictor. In Jurkat, an explicit unpenalized product term supplies much of the improvement previously assigned to a matrix correction. In K562, a substantial rank-one increment remains after fitting either the product or saturation reference and is concentrated around a descriptive mitochondrial gene set. Han spectral gains also persist beyond saturation, with almost all retained improvement orthogonal to the single-product direction. These comparisons identify which specified explanations account for predictive benefit and which leave additional structure to learn.

A bounded-intermediate model offers a conditional explanation for these response patterns: shared capacity-limited processes can turn additive inputs into nonadditive outputs. Local curvature produces product terms, while different perturbation loadings can produce module-associated contrasts.[46] The model also clarifies interpretation. One nonlinear intermediate can generate several response directions, and endpoint elimination preserves relative module differences. Supplementary Note 24 develops the assumptions, rank counterexample and block-contrast identity. The empirical evidence concerns predictive increments and annotated patterns; identifying molecular processes would require measurements that distinguish their mechanisms.

Beyond the source of a response pattern, its repeatability helps assess the training information available to a learner. The functional measurements differed substantially in endpoint-eliminated repeat agreement; high agreement coexisted with negligible gains in low-support K562. Experimental layout, response structure, measurement precision and model capacity jointly determine what can transfer to new combinations. The RNA results provide a complementary example: training-measurement weights retained large useful corrections in a small group of readouts while reducing adverse changes elsewhere. Neither condition count nor output dimension alone summarizes this opportunity.

These differences in learned responses motivate a practical next step: controlling how a correction enters the prediction. A common amplitude links endpoint refinement and partner adjustment even when their validation evidence differs. Component control separates those decisions: retain the endpoint contribution and estimate partner strength from internally held-out combinations. Its endpoint contribution stays fixed while the partner amplitude changes. Shen demonstrates the value of retaining endpoint refinement, and the native Han rank-one comparison shows that internal control can improve an already useful partner prediction. This control uses the fitted predictor and its internal validation outputs.

This work builds on strong-baseline comparisons, Systema's separation of systematic variation and Miller's metric calibration.[10,11,12] WitnessCell connects these lines of work to predictor design: establish which response component a branch can change, learn its increment from permitted measurements, and regulate that increment through internally held-out combinations. Projection, kernel regression and matrix regularization make the operations explicit and testable. The same questions can guide comparisons of representations such as scGPT, scFoundation and Geneformer.[24,25,26]

For researchers completing a partially measured screen, the workflow begins with available single responses and measured combinations. A suitable baseline predicts the requested combinations; an additional learner is evaluated against that baseline using internal whole-combination holdouts. The user can then retain the fitted endpoint contribution and apply the internally chosen strength to the partner correction on a specified query panel. The current functional evidence concerns screens with substantial combination support, and the RNA gain concerns a concentrated group of readouts. Targeted RNA and multimodal screens offer further settings for evaluating these capabilities,[27,28,29] while informative selection of joint measurements connects the target-information analysis to experiment planning.[30,31] Within efforts toward predictive cellular models,[32,33] WitnessCell provides a way to predict added responses, establish their value and control their use.

## Methods

### Study design and implementation identities

The study combines reanalysis of public perturbation measurements with a specified synthetic causal system. The primary response method is the released WitnessCell transcriptomic core. The matched RNA partner predictor, functional growth model, analytic-risk predictor and empirical risk ranking are separately fitted implementations. WitnessCell–PairPick names the partner-selection application; its default response configuration is the archived R-G algorithm. PairPick-Stable and PairPick-TargetRank retain their separate identities. Model identifiers in the source archives are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Biological prediction splits assign unordered perturbation pairs, keeping associated cells, guides, orientations and repeated measurements together. Permitted singles remain available. Mixed activation/repression tasks additionally group mode variants by physical gene pair. Inner fitting reconstructs preprocessing, baseline and correction from the allowed observations. Supplementary Table S30 records development exposure, procedure and prediction freezes, and source relationships for each evaluation; Supplementary Note 11 reports original outcomes and subsequent amendments. Training and scoring were separated locally. Dependence among seeds, folds, shared-source datasets and algebraic contrasts is handled as specified under statistical reporting below.

### Data sources and response definitions

CRISPR repression and activation permit complementary genetic interventions.[34] Pooled single-cell assays link perturbation identities to molecular responses,[21,35,36,37] and genome-scale Perturb-seq provides broader gene-function context.[38] These assay precedents motivate the response definitions below; the benchmark sources are specified separately.

The released benchmark used scPerturBench-processed Norman, Wessels, Schmidt and Replogle_exp6 datasets, their Gene Ontology support filters and public split seeds 1–3.[9] Primary experimental sources include Norman GSE133344, Wessels GSE213957 and Replogle GSE146194; Schmidt retains the benchmark's processed labels with the primary T-cell study as provenance.[1,16,2,17] Benchmark and matched CaRPool analyses share the Wessels source and constitute different tasks, not independent source replication. The full benchmark contains 540 combination–seed and 114 unseen-single records.

RNA effects are condition means relative to the permitted control reference. Source preprocessing and gene axes are retained for each experiment. The matched CaRPool task has 55 pairs, fourteen singles, five outer folds, eleven same-fit evaluation rectangles and 4,995 non-target readouts. Its neural comparators retain their 5,020-gene model axis and use the same scoring mask. Growth phenotypes are repeat-specific Triple Sequencing measurements from GSE116198.[3] Independent checks use Dixit GSE90063/GSM2396860 and Combi-seq GSE174695.[21,22] The mixed-mode selection application uses GSE220974.[23]

### Single-response estimation and the released transcriptomic core

For a measured single $g$, the effect is $e_g=\mu_g-\mu_0$. Missing endpoints use a head combining the equal-condition mean of training singles, a ten-neighbour Jaccard-weighted Gene Ontology response and a self-response anchor. Leave-one-single feature construction excludes the endpoint being fitted. Coefficients use numerical ridge $10^{-8}$ and clipping to $[0,2]$. The additional on-target amplitude head uses generic self response and sign-matched tail and root-mean-square fingerprint features, relative ridge 0.2 and coefficient bounds $[-1.5,1.5]$.

For every outer training endpoint, nested checks remove that endpoint before fitting the base head and amplitude correction. Activation requires positive one-sided 95% lower bounds for both relative all-gene MSE gain and top-100 correlation gain; top-100 MSE is recorded but is not a third gate. With fewer than six usable endpoints, the preceding head is retained. Measured endpoints are unchanged. This amplitude extension is excluded from selection of the released combination hyperparameters.

With $e^{\mathrm{base}}$ and $e^{\mathrm{final}}$ denoting the preceding and final endpoint effects, the released predictor is

$$\widehat y_q=\mu_0+S_\alpha(e_g^{\mathrm{final}}+e_h^{\mathrm{final}})+\gamma K^{\mathrm{end}}_{QT}(K^{\mathrm{end}}_{TT}+\eta I)^{-1}R_T,$$

where $S_\alpha(z)=z/(1+\alpha|z|)$ acts coordinatewise, $K^{\mathrm{end}}_{TT}=X_TX_T^\top/2$ and $K^{\mathrm{end}}_{QT}=X_QX_T^\top/2$. Training residuals $R_T$ subtract $\mu_0+S_\alpha(e_g^{\mathrm{base}}+e_h^{\mathrm{base}})$. This conservative-residual/final-backbone convention reproduces the released implementation; endpoint versions coincide when their singles are measured.

Validation selects $\alpha\in\{0,.02,.05,.1,.2,.5\}$ and $\eta\in\{.01,.03,.1,.3,1\}$. For transferred correction $D$ and remaining validation error $E$, the amplitude is $\gamma=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\langle D,E\rangle/\|D\|_F^2$, with zero for zero correction energy. MSE ties favour smaller $\alpha$, then smaller $\eta$. The original use of validation combinations is retained and distinguished from later extensions selected entirely inside their outer training sets.

### Benchmark aggregation and response losses

The formal comparison reconstructs the released top-100 six-metric aggregation over all four datasets. GEARS uses disjoint mode-labelled copies of its perturbation graph and a physical-gene coexpression graph inferred from permitted controls and singles. Scouter uses direct-sum GenePT embeddings, activation $[e,0]$ and repression $[0,e]$. These are explicit mixed-mode adapters of the upstream architectures.

GEARS and Scouter each complete five folds and seeds 0, 1 and 2. GEARS uses Adam with learning rate .001, weight decay .0005, batch size 32, and the two schedule/cap combinations described above. Scouter uses Adam .001, batch size 256 and exponential learning-rate factor .9, with forty epochs and the predefined optional extension. Both select on inner fold zero by rectangle MSE, then non-target RNA MSE and earlier epoch; final fits restart for the selected duration. The main comparison scores the arithmetic mean of three seed predictions. It does not select the best seed or average seed-specific choice losses.

Some other models' outer results had been viewed before neural completion. GEARS and Scouter retained the fixed budgets and selection rules during completion. This history is reported as a retrospective source comparison, not a wholly untouched preregistered confirmation. Paired uncertainty resamples the eight physical anchors and is descriptive of the observed candidate pools. Full RNA, projected partner and recommendation losses remain separate endpoints.

### Independent-source checks and statistical reporting

The frozen Dixit RNA evaluation uses 45 pairs, ten permitted TF singles and 23,519 non-target readouts. Its extension is selected using training observations and compared with its own $M_0$ baseline. The frozen Combi-seq evaluation includes 374 drug pairs, 36 endpoints and 51 program genes, with its specified endpoint reference. Their gains are not pooled across tasks or combined with later development analyses. Target-specific one-measurement acquisition results remain archived separately: the positive development finding did not persist on six outer targets, and no outer target is removed from that decision.

Effect estimates retain the original unit, comparator, readout mask and aggregation. Bootstrap resampling follows the empirical-distribution principle,[42] using the dependence units specified here. Pair-bootstrap intervals cluster repeated records by canonical pair; selection intervals cluster by physical anchor; synthetic intervals quantify Monte Carlo variability across noise seeds. Readouts, cell halves, repeated fitted seeds and overlapping rectangles are not substituted for independent experimental units. The fifteen comparator intervals are pointwise, conditional on saved scores; they do not provide simultaneous coverage or account for the complete model-development history. Overlapping RNA rectangles are not used as independent Bernoulli trials for sign-test significance. Descriptive examples and sensitivity analyses supplement aggregate results without replacing them.

### Computational reproducibility

The code archive records source identities, permitted inputs, model selections, predictions and scoring rules. Compact replay reconstructs scores from saved sufficient statistics or frozen arrays according to each task's documented scope. Original-prediction replay, model-weight replay and full retraining are separate operations.

The GSE220974 module contains all thirty final neural weight identities and the complete comparison, including adverse and historical results. Its compact default replay reproduces metrics; complete training additionally requires the original cell matrices, graph and embedding assets and compatible environments. Stochastic training can vary across hardware.

Han native replay reconstructs predictions from saved selected inner and final weights, re-estimates training-side control and verifies all reported outer losses. Its full-refitting entry separately executes the frozen learning and selection procedure. Independent QR residualization checks the reported SSEs and control coefficients. Native Han fitting starts from the archived phenotype matrices; reconstruction from compact sequencing counts remains available in the base code release. The experiment package documents numerical environments, accepted tolerances and the cache correction completed before scoring. Historical frozen-weight replay retains its own documented scope.
